## What Does "Default Alive" vs. "Default Dead" Actually Mean?

<p>Here's the simple version:</p>

<p><strong>You're default alive</strong> when, at today's growth rate and spending, you're on track to reach profitability before you run out of cash. You don't <em>need</em> new money for this to work: your business does.</p>

<p><strong>You're default dead</strong> when, at today's trajectory, you'll run out of cash before you hit profitability. Your current path requires outside capital or a change in direction.</p>

<p><strong>Both are forecasts, not fate.</strong></p>

<p>Paul Graham's <a href="https://paulgraham.com/aord.html" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">original essay</a> explains this brilliantly. But most people miss the point.</p>

<p>A default alive startup can still be burning cash today. Default dead doesn't mean you're bankrupt. It means the numbers say you need to change course before it's too late.</p>

## The Four Inputs You Need

<p>You don't need a fancy financial model. Paul Graham's original test asked a simple question: if your revenue grows and expenses stay flat, will you get to profitability before your cash runs out?</p>

<p>That's four numbers.</p>

### 1. Available cash

<p>How much cash can you actually spend right now?</p>

<p>Include money that's available across your operating accounts. But leave out restricted cash, invoices you haven't collected, uncommitted financing, and any funds you can't or won't be able to legally spend.</p>

<p><strong>Example: $300,000</strong></p>

<p>That's your runway. Your buffer. Your margin for error.</p>

### 2. Current monthly revenue

<p>How much money is coming into the business month over month?</p>

<p>Here's where people trip up: don't confuse annual recurring revenue (ARR) with actual cash collections.</p>

<p>If you're a SaaS company and close a $120,000 annual contract, that's $10,000 a month on paper. But depending on when that payment comes in and how many times a year customers renew, your actual cash timing could be very different.</p>

<p>For our working example, we'll assume:</p>

<p><strong>Monthly revenue: $50,000</strong></p>

### 3. Recent monthly revenue growth

<p>How fast has revenue actually been growing?</p>

<p>Don't use your best month. Use several months to get a representative picture. If you grew from $40,000 to $50,000 over four months, your compound monthly growth rate is:</p>

<p>Monthly growth = (50,000 ÷ 40,000)<sup>1/4</sup> − 1 ≈ 5.7%</p>

<p>Think of growth as an assumption, not a promise. Test different scenarios: what if growth slows to 3%? What if a key customer churns? What if collections lag?</p>

### 4. Monthly operating expenses

<p>How much does it cost to run the business?</p>

<p>Payroll, software, infrastructure, contractors, rent, marketing: put it all in. For a more accurate picture, also include taxes, debt payments, and planned hires with their actual payment dates.</p>

<p><strong>Example: $90,000</strong></p>

<p>With these four numbers, you can project when you'll break even, and whether you'll survive until then.</p>

## How to Calculate: Your Default Alive or Default Dead Status

<p>This isn't a simple ratio. It's a forward-looking cash projection.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">You're looking for two dates:</p>

<ol>
  <li>When revenue covers expenses (break-even)</li>
  <li>When your cash runs out</li>
</ol>

<p><strong>If the break-even date comes first → Default alive</strong></p>

<p><strong>If the cash runs out first → Default dead</strong></p>

### Step 1: Project monthly revenue

<p>Assuming your growth rate holds steady, your revenue in month m would be:</p>

<p>Revenue<sub>m</sub> = Current revenue × (1 + growth rate)<sup>m−1</sup></p>

<p>Month 1 is next month. If you start at $50,000 and grow 10% monthly, you get $55,000 in month 2, then $60,500 in month 3, and so on.</p>

### Step 2: Project expenses

<p>In Graham's simplified version, expenses stay flat. But in the real world, they don't. New hires? Their salaries. Contract renewals? Their costs. Growth might mean higher marketing or sales commissions.</p>

<p>Use consistent time periods and assumptions for both revenue and expenses.</p>

### Step 3: Calculate monthly cash flow

<p>If you're a cash-based business (collections happen in the month the sale closes):</p>

<p><strong>Monthly net cash flow = Cash receipts − Cash payments</strong></p>

<p><strong>Ending cash = Starting cash + Monthly net cash flow</strong></p>

<p>When receipts are lower than expenses, cash drains. When receipts beat expenses, cash grows. Repeat for each month in your forecast.</p>

### Step 4: Find your break-even month

<p>When does recurring revenue finally cover your operating expenses?</p>

<p>In our example, that's month 8 when revenue hits around $90,000.</p>

<p><strong>Important:</strong> Accounting profit ≠ cash availability. Taxes, working capital changes, and financing payments all mess with the timing.</p>

### Step 5: Find the first cash shortfall

<p>This is where most people go wrong. Don't just check the ending balance.</p>

<p>A company might have cash through month 8, but what if they need to pay a $150,000 vendor bill on the 15th, but a key customer doesn't pay until the 25th?</p>

<p>They're "fine" at month-end, but they can't actually pay the bill.</p>

<p>Use weekly or daily cash dates for accuracy. Model the timing of real payments and receipts.</p>

### Step 6: Compare

<p>If break-even comes before the first cash shortfall → <strong>default alive</strong></p>

<p>If cash runs out before break-even → <strong>default dead</strong></p>

<p><strong>And here's the kicker:</strong> When your forecast is close to the boundary, don't assume you're safe. Test conservative scenarios. What if growth slows? What if collections lag?</p>

## The Founder's Default Alive Checklist

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Use this checklist in three situations:</p>

<ul>
  <li>During your monthly financial review</li>
  <li>Before making a major hiring decision</li>
  <li>When investors ask whether your company is "fundable"</li>
</ul>

<ul>
  <li><strong>1. Verify your available cash.</strong> Reconcile balances. Exclude restricted cash, uncleared deposits, and anything you can't actually spend.</li>
  <li><strong>2. Validate your revenue.</strong> Separate recognized revenue from actual cash collected. Factor in unpaid invoices and any irregular customers.</li>
  <li><strong>3. Calculate realized growth.</strong> Use recent representative months. Factor in churn, seasonality, and concentrate on recurring business, not one-off deals.</li>
  <li><strong>4. Forecast expenses.</strong> Payroll, vendors, software, rent, marketing, and committed hiring. For accuracy, model payment dates.</li>
  <li><strong>5. Find break-even.</strong> When will recurring revenue cover operating expenses? Distinguish accounting profit from cash flow.</li>
  <li><strong>6. Find first shortfall.</strong> Project cash through break-even and note the first negative balance. Watch the minimum cash balance along the way, not just the ending number.</li>
  <li><strong>7. Stress-test.</strong> What if growth slows? What if collections lag? What if a key customer churns? How does the forecast change?</li>
  <li><strong>8. Write a no-fundraise Plan B.</strong> What would you do if investors don't move? Who owns the changes? What's the last date you can delay?</li>
  <li><strong>9. Recalculate monthly.</strong> And again whenever something changes: new hire, big win, lost customer, changed terms, financing decision.</li>
</ul>

### Three questions that matter most

<p>Once you've run through the checklist, ask yourself:</p>

<p><strong>When do we become profitable?</strong></p>

<p><strong>When could we first run short of cash?</strong></p>

<p><strong>What's the last safe date to change our plan?</strong></p>

<p>If you can't answer all three, stop. Your forecast needs work before you make real decisions.</p>

## Why Your "Runway" Number Might Be Misleading

<p>Founders know their runway. They'll say, "We have 12 months of runway."</p>

<p>But that number often tells you more about how you calculated it than what it actually means.</p>

### Runway assumes the burn rate stays the same

<p><strong>Runway = Available cash / Monthly net burn</strong></p>

<p>In our example: $300,000 / $40,000 = 7.5 months.</p>

<p>But that assumes nothing changes. And things <em>will</em> change. If revenue grows and expenses stay flat, your net burn drops. You might reach profitability with months to spare.</p>

<p><strong>Runway measures your cash at today's burn.</strong> It's backward-looking.</p>

### Burn multiple measures growth efficiency, not survival

<p>David Sacks introduced burn multiple to measure capital efficiency:</p>

<p><strong>Burn multiple = Net burn during a period / Net new ARR in that period</strong></p>

<p>If you burned $600,000 in Q1 and added $300,000 in net new ARR, your burn multiple is 2.0x. You spent two dollars of cash to add one dollar of growth.</p>

<p>Lower is better when you're growing. If your new ARR is zero, the metric breaks down.</p>

<p>But here's what I think gets missed:</p>

<p><strong>Burn multiple doesn't tell you when you'll survive. It tells you how efficiently you're burning.</strong></p>

<p>A company can have a 1.5x burn multiple and still be default dead if growth is too slow to reach profitability in time. A company can be default alive even with a 3x burn multiple if they're close to profitability.</p>

<p><strong>Runway measures cash duration. Burn multiple measures capital efficiency. Default alive measures whether your business can survive on its own trajectory.</strong></p>

<p>You need all three numbers to make sense.</p>

## The Cash Timing Problem: Your "Safe" Month Hides a Crisis

<p>I've seen this play out too many times.</p>

<p>A startup comes to me saying, "Our month-end cash balance is positive. We're fine."</p>

<p>Here's what I found:</p>

<p>They started the month with $20,000. They expected $60,000 in customer payments. They had $50,000 in payroll coming up.</p>

<p>Month-end projection: $20,000 + $60,000 − $50,000 = $30,000. "We're good."</p>

<p>Reality: The payroll is due on the 15th. The customer payment comes on the 25th.</p>

## What To Do If The Answer Is "Default Dead"

<p>Here's the hard truth: finding out you're default dead sucks.</p>

<p>But the earlier you know it, the more time you have to do something about it.</p>

<p>Your goal isn't to make your runway number look better. Your goal is to change your trajectory.</p>

### 1. Cut spending that isn't essential

<p>Look at every recurring expense. Is this software actually used? Does this contractor deliver value? Will this marketing spend actually generate customers? What about planned hiring that's justified by future revenue that might never materialize?</p>

<p>There's a difference between costs that keep the engine running and costs that the engine hopes to carry.</p>

### 2. Fix your cash collection

<p>You can improve cash availability without changing sales.</p>

<p>Look at: How quickly do customers pay? What's our overdue balance? Are we getting paid upfront or net-30/60/90?</p>

<p>For subscription businesses: is growth coming in collected cash or just reported ARR? I've seen startups get carried away by top-line ARR numbers only to discover half of it doesn't actually convert to cash.</p>

### 3. Reduce expenses to meet the new reality

<p>Going back to our example: cut expenses from $90,000 to $70,000 and 3% growth now gets you to break-even by month 13.</p>

<p>Cost cuts alone won't solve every problem, but they can fundamentally shift the math in your favor.</p>

### 4. Treat fundraising as a choice, not a guarantee

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">If you're default dead, you need to start thinking about raising money. But here's what I wish more people understood:</p>

<ul>
  <li>A SAFE that hasn't been signed isn't money</li>
  <li>A bridge round that depends on "getting a larger round" isn't secured capital</li>
  <li>A loan that depends on a certain balance sheet isn't accessible capital</li>
</ul>

<p>Build two forecasts:</p>

<p><strong>Base case (no new money):</strong> Your current trajectory. Know when you hit bottom.</p>

<p><strong>Funded scenario:</strong> Realistic timing and amount of capital. Know what it costs and what new obligations you're taking on.</p>

<p>Decide how many months you can operate in the base case before activating the funded scenario.</p>

### 5. Set a decision deadline

<p>You might be able to delay changes, but don't delay the decision.</p>

<p>If your cash runs out in eight weeks, you need to know now what you're committing to. Work backward from your threshold and your obligations.</p>

## The "Fatal Pinch": A Silent Trap

<p>Paul Graham had a name for this: the <em>fatal pinch</em>.</p>

<p>It's three conditions combined:</p>

<p><strong>Default dead + slow growth + no time to fix it.</strong></p>

<p>I've watched companies sit for months, convinced the next fundraise will save them. The cash drains. Growth stutters. And then, suddenly, they can't get money anymore.</p>

<p>The market, investors, even your own traction: they've changed.</p>

<p>By the time the numbers finally look bad, it's often too late. The options for fixing things have disappeared.</p>

<p>This is why checking your default-alive status early matters. You're not predicting doom. You're buying time to make choices.</p>

## How Often Should You Re-run This?

<p>Monthly. At minimum.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">And whenever something changes:</p>

<ul>
  <li>New hire or major spending commitment</li>
  <li>Big customer win or unexpected churn</li>
  <li>Growth accelerates or stalls</li>
  <li>Payment terms or collections change</li>
  <li>You make a financing decision</li>
  <li>A significant change in infrastructure or operating costs</li>
</ul>

<p>The longer-term "when will we be profitable" projection and the 13-week "what's coming next" forecast serve different purposes.</p>

<p><strong>Long-term projection:</strong> Can we become sustainable?</p>

<p><strong>Short-term forecast:</strong> Can we survive the next 90 days?</p>

<p>When liquidity is tight, review your 13-week forecast weekly. Stress the scenarios. What if we lose our two biggest customers? What if growth slows to zero?</p>
