## What Does a Finished Cash Flow Spreadsheet Look Like?

<p>Picture this: 13 weekly columns. Each week has:</p>

<p><strong>The core math is simple:</strong></p>

<p>Closing cash = Opening cash + Cash received − Cash paid</p>

<p>Each week's closing balance becomes the next week's opening balance. That's the chain that makes this actually useful.</p>

<p>This isn't your P&L statement, remember? A P&L shows revenue when you earn it (accrual accounting). This forecast shows cash when it actually hits your bank. That difference can be the difference between making payroll and paying out of thin air.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> You might record $80k in revenue but only collect $35k from customers. Still gotta pay the team that Tuesday, even if that payment doesn't hit the bank until Friday.</p>

<p>That timing mismatch? The 13-week forecast makes it visible.</p>

### Separate what's real from what's hopeful

<p>Not all forecasted dollars are created equal.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Actual:</strong> The money's already in the bank.</li>
  <li><strong>Committed:</strong> A contract says it's coming on this date, but timing might shift.</li>
  <li><strong>Expected:</strong> A customer told you they'll pay then and there.</li>
  <li><strong>Assumed:</strong> You're guessing based on historical patterns.</li>
</ul>

<p>A $40k payment from a client who's been late isn't the same as $40k that's already cleared. Keep that distinction clear in your spreadsheet.</p>

## How to Build the Forecast: 8 Simple Steps

<p>Let's walk through it. You can either build it in a blank Excel workbook or use the <a href="/tools/13-week-cash-flow-template" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline"><strong>free template</strong></a> and skip the typing.</p>

### Step 1: Set up your 13 weeks

<p>Open Excel. In cell A4, type Line Item.</p>

<p>Across B4 to N4, enter Week 1 through Week 13.</p>

<p>In B5, type the Monday when your forecast starts.</p>

<p>Then in C5, enter this:</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">=B5+7</code></p>

<p>Drag it across to N5. You've got 13 weekly columns, each starting one week after the last.</p>

<p>Format row 5 as dates. I can't tell you how many times I've saved hours by using actual dates instead of "Week 1, Week 2" labels. Dates make it easier to map payroll, tax deadlines, and customer payment histories.</p>

<p><em>Quick tip:</em> If your forecast starts mid-week, either compress the first period to the actual start date or just start on the next Monday. Don't accidentally include payments that already cleared before you measured your opening balance.</p>

### Step 2: Enter your starting cash

<p>In B6, enter how much cash you have available right now. Let's say $100,000.</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">100000</code></p>

<p>This is the <strong>only</strong> opening balance you should type in manually.</p>

<p>All other weeks' opening balances will calculate automatically by pulling from the previous week's closing balance.</p>

<p>Here's why: if you manually type Week 4's opening balance, you're disconnecting the model from what actually happened in Weeks 1–3. If the business unexpectedly spends an extra $10k in Week 3, every week after it should update automatically.</p>

### Step 3: Enter your cash inflows

<p>Cash inflows are money that will <em>actually hit your bank account</em>.</p>

<p>Use these rows:</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="The cash-in rows of the sheet."
  columns={['Row', 'Use for']}
  rows={[
    ['8', 'Customer collections'],
    ['9', 'Annual/quarterly contract payments'],
    ['10', 'Other cash inflows'],
  ]}
/>

<p>In B11, type:</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">=SUM(B8:B10)</code></p>

<p>Drag that across to N11.</p>

<p><strong>Important:</strong> <em>An invoice is not a cash receipt.</em></p>

<p>If you send a $20k invoice in Week 2 with 30-day terms, that doesn't mean $20k hits your bank in Week 2. Enter it in the week you realistically expect it to clear.</p>

<p>Look at your customer's payment history. If they consistently pay 10 days late, put it in Week 7, not Week 6. Don't assume they'll pay on the invoice due date. Trust what their actual behavior has been.</p>

<p>For subscription businesses, annual and quarterly contracts get their own row. A $48k annual fee arriving in Week 12 isn't spread across the year. Your revenue recognition is, but the cash arrives in one lump sum.</p>

### Step 4: Enter your cash outflows

<p>Now list everything you pay out.</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="The cash-out rows of the sheet."
  columns={['Row', 'Use for']}
  rows={[
    ['13', 'Payroll and contractors'],
    ['14', 'Payroll taxes and benefits'],
    ['15', 'Rent and utilities'],
    ['16', 'Software and subscriptions'],
    ['17', 'Marketing'],
    ['18', 'Other cash outflows'],
  ]}
/>

<p>In B19:</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">=SUM(B13:B18)</code></p>

<p>Drag across to N19.</p>

<p><strong>Enter payments as positive numbers.</strong> The net cash flow formula will subtract them.</p>

<p><strong>If you pay biweekly:</strong> Enter each payroll amount only in the weeks it actually hits. A $28k biweekly payroll might hit in Weeks 1 and 3, leaving Weeks 2, 4, 6... with no payroll line. That's <em>the point</em>: the forecast should show you where the big cash holes are.</p>

<p><strong>Don't do this:</strong> Don't divide monthly payroll by four and enter it every week. That smoothes the bumps and gives you false security.</p>

<p>Watch out for one-time payments too: annual software renewals, insurance premiums, tax payments. A single year-end payment can create a cash hole in the 13-week window.</p>

### Step 5: Calculate net flow and closing balance

<p>In B20:</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">=B11-B19</code></p>

<p>That's your net cash flow for Week 1. Positive means more came in than went out. Negative means you're spending more than you're collecting.</p>

<p>Next, in B21:</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">=B6+B20</code></p>

<p>That's your closing cash balance for the week: opening balance plus net flow.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong></p>

<BlogTable
  caption="Week 1 of the example: $100,000 opening cash closes at $69,500."
  columns={['Week 1', 'Amount']}
  rows={[
    ['Opening cash', '$100,000'],
    ['Inflows', '$12,000'],
    ['Outflows', '$42,500'],
    ['Net', '−$30,500'],
    [<strong>Closing</strong>, <strong>$69,500</strong>],
  ]}
  numeric={[1]}
/>

<p>You spent more than you collected, but you still have $69.5k at the end of the week. <em>Negative net flow doesn't mean you're broke.</em></p>

### Step 6: Link the weeks

<p>This is where the magic happens.</p>

<p>In C6, enter:</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">=B21</code></p>

<p>Drag that across to N6.</p>

<p>Now Week 2's opening balance automatically takes Week 1's closing. Week 3's opening takes Week 2's, and so on.</p>

<p>Then drag your SUM formulas for inflows and outflows across to columns C through N, and your closing balance formula too.</p>

<p>Now when you change a single input (say, a customer payment moves from Week 5 to Week 7), all the downstream balances recalculate. You can finally see how timing affects your cash position.</p>

### Step 7: Add a cash floor

<p>Here's something I've learned the hard way: <em>a positive bank balance doesn't always mean you can make the bills.</em></p>

<p>If you have $18k in the bank but a $28k payroll due Tuesday, you're already in trouble, even though you don't technically have negative cash.</p>

<p>Set a <a href="/blog/cash-flow-early-warning-system" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">minimum cash buffer</a> (a "floor"). For this example, let's say $25,000.</p>

<p>In B23:</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">25000</code></p>

<p>In B24:</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">{'=IF(B21<$B$23,"BELOW FLOOR","OK")'}</code></p>

<p>Drag that across to N24.</p>

<p>You'll see "OK" as long as your closing balance is at or above $25k. If you dip below, it says "BELOW FLOOR."</p>

<p><em>Note:</em> The dollar signs in $B$23 are important. They lock that cell reference so the formula stays consistent when you copy it across.</p>

<p>This is a warning based on <em>your policy</em>, not a mathematical certainty that you're insolvent. A negative projected balance is the more serious red flag.</p>

### Step 8: Be realistic about dates

<p>Even with perfect formulas, the forecast is only as good as the dates you enter.</p>

<p>Use the date the cash actually moves, not when the invoice is created.</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="When cash actually moves for common forecast items."
  columns={['Event', 'When cash moves']}
  rows={[
    ['$15k invoice in Week 1', 'Week 5 (when payment clears)'],
    ['Biweekly payroll', 'Weeks 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13'],
    ['Quarterly payment', 'Week 8'],
    ['Annual software renewal', 'Week 7'],
    ['Monthly rent', 'The week rent is due'],

/>

<p>A <a href="/blog/what-is-a-13-week-cash-flow-forecast" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">direct-method forecast</a> works because each transaction sits in the period when cash changes hands.</p>

<p><strong>Don't smooth the bumps.</strong> If you have a $10k payment due in Week 4 and a $5k payment due in Week 7, enter them where they're due. Smoothing them to spread out makes your cash look better when it's actually going to be tighter.</p>

### Why this is interesting

<ul>
  <li>Starting cash: $100k</li>
  <li>Ending cash: $42,500</li>
  <li>Minimum floor: $25,000</li>
</ul>

<p>If you only looked at Week 13, you'd think, "I'm fine." The quarter ends with $42.5k, nowhere near disaster.</p>

<p>But the weekly view tells a different story.</p>

<p><strong>Week 7:</strong> Payroll ($28k) + payroll taxes ($6k) + $9k software renewal = $43k out in one week, plus $2.5k marketing and misc. Receipts are only $12k. That's a $33.5k net drain, dropping closing cash to $23,500, right below the floor.</p>

<p><strong>Week 11:</strong> The annual $48k customer payment doesn't hit until Week 12. You're going to end the week with $15,500, which is $9,500 below your $25k floor.</p>

<p>These aren't profitability problems. They're <em>cash timing</em> problems.</p>

### What can you do about it?

<p>You have options:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Negotiate moving that $9k software renewal from Week 7 to Week 8. If they agree, Week 7 ends with $32.5k instead.</li>
  <li>Ask your client to advance the $48k payment to Week 11. That would put you at $63.5k instead of $15.5k.</li>
</ul>

<p>Neither changes the total economics. Just shifts when cash hits.</p>

<p><strong>Important:</strong> Don't move payments around just because you hope you can. Frame these as negotiations: "Could we move the $9k renewal to next week?" and "Could you send the $48k payment a week early?"</p>

<p>This forecast doesn't replace conversation. It helps you have the right conversations at the right time.</p>

## What a Week-End Balance Doesn't Tell You

<p>Here's a nuance a lot of guides skip.</p>

<p><strong>A positive week-ending balance doesn't guarantee you can make payments that week.</strong></p>

<p>Imagine:</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="One week that closes positive but goes $10,000 short on Tuesday."
  columns={['Event', 'Cash balance']}
  rows={[
    ['Monday morning opening', '$20,000'],
    ['Tuesday payroll ($30k)', <strong>−$10,000</strong>],
    ['Friday collection ($40k)', '$30,000'],
    ['Friday closing', '$30,000'],
  ]}
  numeric={[1]}
  negativeRows={[1]}
/>

<p>By Friday, you're fine. But on Tuesday, you're $10k short.</p>

<p>A weekly model will tell you the week ends positive. It won't tell you you have a mid-week cash shortfall.</p>

<p>This matters a lot for:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Payroll dates</li>
  <li>Tax deadlines</li>
  <li>Debt obligations</li>
  <li>Irregular customer payments</li>
</ul>

### When should you forecast daily?

<p>Use daily or transaction-level forecasting when:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Your weekly balance is near your cash floor</li>
  <li>A big payroll or payment comes due before a collection</li>
  <li>You're worried about actually making a payment on a specific day</li>
  <li>You need to know exactly when you hit zero</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Weekly forecasts tell you which weeks are risky. Daily forecasts tell you which days are dangerous.</strong></p>

<p>You might need both.</p>

## How to Update the Forecast Every Monday

<p>A forecast shouldn't live in a folder somewhere from October. It should be a living document.</p>

<p>Every Monday, do this:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Review last week's bank transactions.</strong> What came in? What went out?</li>
  <li><strong>Compare to your forecast.</strong> Did customers pay on time? Were expenses higher or lower than expected?</li>
  <li><strong>Reconcile the cash balance.</strong> Your forecast's Week 1 closing should match the bank (plus/minus timing differences).</li>
  <li><strong>Roll it forward.</strong> Archive Week 1 as actuals. Push Weeks 2–13 to become Weeks 1–12. Add a new Week 13 with updated assumptions.</li>
  <li><strong>Update your opening balance.</strong> Use what's actually in the bank today.</li>
  <li><strong>Refresh assumptions.</strong> Are customers paying later than expected? Are you paying vendors on time? Have expenses changed?</li>
  <li><strong>Check your cash floor.</strong> Where's the earliest breach? What actions can you take?</li>
</ol>

<p>It takes maybe 10–15 minutes. Then your forecast stays relevant.</p>

## Forecast vs. Actual: A Quick Variance Check

<p>Here's how to measure whether your assumptions were right.</p>

<p>Say you forecasted $20k in collections but only received $12k. That's an <strong>−$8k variance</strong>.</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="A forecast-versus-actual check for one week: collections came in $8,000 short."
  columns={['Item', 'Forecast', 'Actual', 'Variance']}
  rows={[
    ['Customer collections', '$20,000', '$12,000', '−$8,000'],

  ]}
  numeric={[1, 2, 3]}
/>

<p>The forecast assumed $20k came in. It didn't. Your closing cash is $8k lower than planned.</p>

<p>You can calculate variance directly (actual minus forecast):</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">=C2-B2</code></p>

<p><strong>What do you do with it?</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>If the customer still owes the $8k and says they'll pay next week, move that expected receipt forward.</li>
  <li>If they're not going to pay it at all, remove or mark it as uncollectible.</li>
  <li>Update your forward-looking balances.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Don't just leave $8k uncollected in the completed week and pretend it doesn't matter.</strong> Variance analysis forces you to confront reality, which makes your model more credible going forward.</p>

<p>Over time, you'll spot patterns: "This client consistently pays 2 weeks late." That becomes part of your updated assumptions.</p>

### Quick tests

<p><strong>Test 1: Opening-closing link</strong></p>

<p>In a blank cell, type:</p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">=C6-B21</code></p>

<p>Should return 0. If not, your Week 2 opening doesn't match Week 1's closing.</p>

<p><strong>Test 2: Closing calculation</strong></p>

<p><code className="rounded bg-muted px-1.5 py-0.5 text-sm">=B21-(B6+B20)</code></p>

<p>Should also be 0. That's your closing formula check.</p>

<p><strong>Test 3: Cash floor alert</strong></p>

<p>Temporarily lower a key inflow so Week 1 closing drops below your floor. The status cell should turn to "BELOW FLOOR." Fix your input and restore.</p>

<p><strong>Test 4: Timing shift</strong></p>

<p>Move a receipt from Week 3 to Week 4. Week 3's closing should drop by that amount. Week 4's closing should end up where it was. Week 5+ shouldn't change (assuming no downstream effects).</p>

<p>These quick tests will catch broken formulas, wrong references, or manual overrides that will eat your credibility.</p>

## Common Forecasting Mistakes

<p>I've seen these a dozen times, including on my own forecasts:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Treating invoices as cash.</strong> Invoice ≠ cash. Forecast when it <em>hits the bank</em>.</li>
  <li><strong>Smoothing expenses.</strong> Don't divide monthly rent or annual subscriptions across weeks. Enter them on their due dates.</li>
  <li><strong>Double-counting receipts.</strong> An annual contract shouldn't appear under both "customers" and "contracts."</li>
  <li><strong>Forgetting annual payments.</strong> Insurance, domain renewals, software: these sneak up on you.</li>
  <li><strong>Assuming perfect payment timing.</strong> Your client says they'll pay in 30 days. They've been 45 days late the last six times.</li>
  <li><strong>Overwriting calculated cells.</strong> Typing a number over a formula breaks the chain. Protect your formula ranges.</li>
  <li><strong>Ignoring intraweek timing.</strong> Week 13 ends with $50k but a $40k payroll is due Tuesday. Are you really safe?</li>
  <li><strong>Not updating.</strong> A forecast from last month that you've never touched is a paperweight.</li>
</ol>
