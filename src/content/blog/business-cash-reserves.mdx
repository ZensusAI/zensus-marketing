<p id="blog-article-lead" className="text-lg leading-relaxed text-muted-foreground">Ask how much cash your business should keep and you'll get two stock answers: three to six months of expenses, or 10 to 30% of annual revenue. For the same company, those rules can point to amounts hundreds of thousands of dollars apart.</p>

Neither rule looks at when your money actually leaves. This guide compares the common rules, then shows a method built on your own calendar: the heaviest four weeks of payments you'll face, plus the customer payment most likely to arrive late. It's written for growing businesses with a payroll, roughly 5 to 50 people.

## Quick Answer

Most advice says to keep three to six months of operating expenses in reserve. A more precise floor is the total of your heaviest four weeks of payments, often two payrolls, January taxes and annual bills, plus your largest customer payment that could arrive late. Keep more on top if raising extra cash would take you weeks, or if your revenue is seasonal.

## What is a business cash reserve?

A business cash reserve is money you keep available to cover payments when income is late, lower than expected or stops for a while. It is separate from the cash you plan to spend this month.

The term also names a few unrelated things you may see in search results: savings products some banks and apps call "cash reserve" accounts, and the reserves banks themselves hold, a topic from economics classes. This guide is about the first meaning, the buffer your business keeps for itself.

## The common rules of thumb, compared

Each rule is a reasonable starting point. Each also misses something.

| Rule | Where it comes from | What it gets right | What it misses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 to 6 months of operating expenses | Banks and advisers, including <a href="https://smallbusinessresources.wf.com/small-business-cash-reserves-tips/" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Wells Fargo</a> and <a href="https://www.score.org/resource/blog-post/how-much-cash-should-a-small-business-keep-reserve" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">SCORE</a> | Simple, and scales with your costs | Treats every month as equal, when payroll and tax weeks are not |
| 9 to 12 months of expenses | Wells Fargo's advice for seasonal or downturn-sensitive businesses | Accounts for long quiet periods | Can tie up cash a growing business needs |
| 10 to 30% of annual revenue | <a href="https://anderscpa.com/learn/blog/business-cash-requirements/" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Anders CPA</a>, 10% for stable businesses up to 30% for fast-growing or unpredictable ones | Links the reserve to risk | Revenue isn't spending; two firms with equal revenue can have very different costs |
| Enough to last until new money arrives | SCORE: about 3 to 5 days for your own funds, two months for a bank loan, 6 to 9 months for angel investment | Ties the reserve to how fast you could raise cash | Doesn't say what your next few weeks will cost |

For context, the typical small business holds far less than any of these. A 2016 <a href="https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/business-growth-and-entrepreneurship/report-cash-flows-balances-and-buffer-days" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">JPMorgan Chase Institute study</a> of 597,000 small businesses found a median cash buffer of 27 days, meaning half could cover less than a month of payments if their income stopped. A quarter had less than 13 days.

## A better method: start from your heaviest four weeks

Instead of averaging your costs, look at when they land. Most businesses have a few weeks each year where several large bills arrive together. Your reserve has to survive those weeks, even if a customer pays late at the same time.

That gives you two numbers.

**Your cash floor** is the least you should ever hold. Work it out in four steps:

1. **List your payments by date for the next 12 months.** Include payroll on each pay date, employer payroll taxes, rent, benefits, insurance, loan payments, and any annual or quarterly bills. To see which of these count as overhead, read <a href="/blog/what-is-overhead" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">what overhead is</a>.
2. **Find the four weeks with the most going out.** For many businesses that's January: two payrolls with employer taxes at their highest, plus annual renewals.
3. **Add your largest customer payment that could arrive late.** Use one regular payment from your biggest customer, or the invoice that's late most often.
4. **The total is your floor.** Below it, one late payment in your busiest weeks could leave you short.

**Your reserve target** is the floor plus extra time to raise cash. If you could draw on a line of credit within days, the floor may be enough. If new money would take months, from a loan or investors, hold more on top, as SCORE's lead times suggest.

This is the same idea a 13-week cash flow forecast is built on. If you already keep one, steps 1 and 2 are done: read off the four heaviest weeks. **How the rules compare for this business**

| Rule | Reserve it suggests |
| --- | ---: |
| JPMorgan median: 27 days of spending | About $92,600 |

For this team, the floor works out to about six weeks of average spending. The typical buffer of 27 days would not survive January if the largest customer paid late. The three-to-six-month rule would be safe but would hold back $160,000 to $470,000 more than the floor, money a growing business could put to work. Whatever extra it holds above the floor should match how long it would take to raise more cash.

## How the right reserve changes by business type

The method stays the same; what changes is which weeks are heaviest and how much extra to hold.

| Business type | What drives the floor | What to adjust |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seasonal business | Payroll and fixed costs during the quiet months | Size the floor for your slowest stretch, not an average month; Wells Fargo suggests up to 9 to 12 months of expenses for highly seasonal firms |
| SaaS with annual contracts | Months between big renewals, when cash only goes out | Find the longest gap between renewals and cover it; see <a href="/blog/arr-vs-cash-for-founders" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">ARR vs cash flow</a> |
| Agency or services firm | Clients paying 30 to 60 days late while payroll runs on time | Add your two latest-paying clients, not one; see <a href="/blog/cash-flow-forecasting-for-agencies" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">cash flow forecasting for agencies</a> |
| Startup not yet profitable | Monthly burn, with no profit to refill the reserve | Your reserve is your runway; track your <a href="/blog/zero-cash-date-for-founders" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">zero cash date</a> and start raising well before it |

## How to build the reserve, and where to keep it

If you're below your floor today, build up to it before anything else that's optional:

- **Set aside a fixed share of each customer payment** into a separate account, so the reserve grows without a decision each time.
- **Hold back part of strong months** instead of spending them; seasonal businesses especially.
- **Arrange a line of credit while things are good.** It's easier to get before you need it, and it shortens how long you'd wait for extra cash.

Keep the reserve where you can reach it within a few days: a separate business savings or money market account at your bank, rather than money tied up for months. Two practical points:

- **Keep it separate from your operating account**, so it isn't spent by accident.
- **Watch deposit insurance limits.** The FDIC insures $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each ownership category. Accounts owned by a corporation or partnership are insured separately from the owners' personal accounts, while a sole proprietor's business account is counted with the owner's personal accounts (<a href="https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">FDIC</a>). If your reserve grows past that, ask your bank about options to spread it.

This isn't investment advice; for anything beyond a savings account, talk to your accountant or bank.

A reserve only helps if you notice before you dip into it. Put your floor into your cash flow forecast as a line, and check each week whether any week ahead falls below it. If one does, you have time to act: chase an invoice, delay a purchase, or draw on credit.

## The Bottom Line

Three to six months of expenses is a safe default, but it's a guess. A better floor is your heaviest four weeks of payments plus your largest payment that could arrive late, with more on top if raising cash would take time. Work it out once from your forecast, write the number down, and watch for any week that falls below it.
