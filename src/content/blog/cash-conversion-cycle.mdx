  The cash conversion cycle measures how long cash is tied up in a company's operating cycle before it comes back as cash collected from customers.

<p>For a startup, the important question is not simply whether the number is high or low. It is where cash is getting stuck.</p>

<ul>
  <li>How quickly are we turning inventory into sales?</li>
  <li>How quickly are customers paying us?</li>
  <li>How long can we wait before paying suppliers?</li>
</ul>

## What is the cash conversion cycle?

<p>The cash conversion cycle is the number of days between paying cash for operating inputs and collecting cash from customers.</p>

<p>The metric is also called the cash cycle or net operating cycle. It focuses on how long working capital remains tied up in the business.</p>

<p>The standard formula is:</p>

<p><strong>CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO</strong></p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Where:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO)</strong>: how long inventory sits before it is sold</li>
  <li><strong>Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)</strong>: how long customers take to pay after a sale</li>
  <li><strong>Days Payable Outstanding (DPO)</strong>: how long you take to pay suppliers</li>
</ul>

<p>
  <a href="https://docs.oracle.com/en/cloud/saas/analytics/25r4/faiae/general-ledger-subject-area-metric-details.html" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Oracle's financial analytics documentation</a> uses the same basic cash cycle structure, combining days sales outstanding, inventory days, and days payable outstanding.
</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">The idea is simple:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Buy inventory</li>
  <li>Hold inventory</li>
  <li>Sell product</li>
  <li>Invoice customer</li>
  <li>Collect cash</li>
</ul>

<p>Supplier payment timing sits underneath this process.</p>

<p>If you pay suppliers before you collect from customers, cash is tied up. If customers pay you before you need to pay suppliers, the business can operate with less working capital.</p>

## Why does the cash conversion cycle matter for startups?

<p>A longer cash conversion cycle generally means more cash is tied up in operations before it returns to the bank.</p>

<p>That matters because a growing company can consume cash even when revenue and gross profit are increasing.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Consider a company growing quickly. It needs to:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Buy more inventory</li>
  <li>Hire more people</li>
  <li>Pay more vendors</li>
  <li>Extend more customer payment terms</li>
  <li>Carry more accounts receivable</li>
</ul>

<p>Revenue may look excellent. The bank balance can still fall.</p>

<p>This is particularly important for startups that are scaling faster than their cash collections. A company that grows from $500,000 to $2 million in annual sales may need substantially more working capital if customers pay slowly and suppliers require payment quickly.</p>

<p>The cash conversion cycle helps explain why. For how ARR can grow while cash stays flat, see <a href="/blog/arr-vs-cash-for-founders" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">ARR vs cash for founders</a>.</p>

## How do you calculate the cash conversion cycle?

<p>Start with the three components.</p>

### 1. Calculate Days Inventory Outstanding

<p>Days Inventory Outstanding measures how long inventory remains in the business before it is sold.</p>

<p>A common formula is:</p>

<p><strong>DIO = Average Inventory ÷ Cost of Goods Sold × Number of Days</strong></p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">For example, assume:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Average Inventory = $100,000</li>
  <li>Annual COGS = $730,000</li>
  <li>Days = 365</li>
</ul>

<p>Then:</p>

<p><strong>DIO = $100,000 ÷ $730,000 × 365 = 50 days</strong></p>

<p>The company is carrying roughly 50 days of inventory.</p>

<p>For a SaaS company with no meaningful physical inventory, DIO may be close to irrelevant. For an ecommerce, hardware, manufacturing, or retail company, it can be one of the biggest drivers of working capital.</p>

### 2. Calculate Days Sales Outstanding

<p>Days Sales Outstanding measures how long it takes a company to collect money after making a credit sale.</p>

<p>A common formula is:</p>

<p><strong>DSO = Average Accounts Receivable ÷ Credit Sales × Number of Days</strong></p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">For example:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Average Accounts Receivable = $120,000</li>
  <li>Annual Credit Sales = $1,095,000</li>
  <li>Days = 365</li>
</ul>

<p>Then:</p>

<p><strong>DSO = $120,000 ÷ $1,095,000 × 365 = 40 days</strong></p>

<p>The company collects its receivables in about 40 days on average.</p>

<p>DSO is especially important for B2B startups because a sale does not become cash when the contract is signed. It becomes cash when the customer pays.</p>

### 3. Calculate Days Payable Outstanding

<p>Days Payable Outstanding measures how long a company takes to pay its suppliers.</p>

<p>A common formula is:</p>

<p><strong>DPO = Average Accounts Payable ÷ Cost of Goods Sold × Number of Days</strong></p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">For example:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Average Accounts Payable = $90,000</li>
  <li>Annual COGS = $730,000</li>
  <li>Days = 365</li>
</ul>

<p>Then:</p>

<p><strong>DPO = $90,000 ÷ $730,000 × 365 = 45 days</strong></p>

<p>The company takes about 45 days to pay suppliers.</p>

### 4. Put the three numbers together

<p>Now calculate CCC:</p>

<p><strong>CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO = 50 + 40 − 45 = 45 days</strong></p>

<p>The business has a 45-day cash conversion cycle.</p>

## What does a 45-day cash conversion cycle mean?

<p>A 45-day CCC means the business has, on average, about 45 days of cash tied up between paying for operating inputs and collecting customer cash.</p>

<p>Think of it as a funding gap.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">The company:</p>

<ul>
  <li>pays suppliers</li>
  <li>holds inventory</li>
  <li>makes sales</li>
  <li>waits for customers to pay</li>
</ul>

<p>During that period, the company has to fund the gap from somewhere.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">That funding can come from:</p>

<ul>
  <li>existing cash</li>
  <li>supplier credit</li>
  <li>customer prepayments</li>
  <li>working capital facilities</li>
  <li>equity financing</li>
  <li>debt</li>
</ul>

<p>This is why CCC matters to cash planning. A lower CCC generally reduces the amount of working capital the company needs to support a given level of operations.</p>

## What is the difference between CCC, DIO, DSO, and DPO?

<p>These metrics describe different parts of the same cash cycle.</p>

| Metric | What it measures | Founder question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DIO | Time inventory is held | How quickly are we selling what we bought? |
| DSO | Time to collect receivables | How quickly are customers paying? |
| DPO | Time to pay suppliers | How long do we retain cash before paying vendors? |

<p>The key is not to look at CCC alone. A 45-day CCC can come from very different operating models.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">For example:</p>

<ul>
  <li>DIO = 50, DSO = 40, DPO = 45 → CCC = 45 days</li>
  <li>DIO = 10, DSO = 80, DPO = 45 → CCC = 45 days</li>
</ul>

<p>The headline number is identical. The operating problem is completely different.</p>

<p>The first company has an inventory problem. The second has a collections problem.</p>

## Is a lower cash conversion cycle always better?

<p>Generally, a shorter CCC means less cash is tied up in the operating cycle, but the right target depends on the business model.</p>

<p>A company should not reduce CCC at any cost.</p>

<p>Pushing suppliers to accept extremely short payment terms could reduce DPO and make cash flow worse. Offering customers aggressive early-payment discounts could reduce DSO while hurting gross margin.</p>

<p>The objective is not to make every metric as small or large as possible. The objective is to improve the economics and timing of the entire operating cycle.</p>

## What does a negative cash conversion cycle mean?

<p>A negative cash conversion cycle means a company collects cash from customers before it has to pay its suppliers.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">For example:</p>

<ul>
  <li>DIO = 20 days</li>
  <li>DSO = 5 days</li>
  <li>DPO = 40 days</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>CCC = 20 + 5 − 40 = −15 days</strong></p>

<p>In this example, the operating cycle is negative 15 days. The company is effectively collecting customer cash before its supplier payments are due.</p>

<p>That can be a powerful business model advantage.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">It is common to see this structure in businesses where:</p>

<ul>
  <li>customers pay upfront</li>
  <li>inventory turns quickly</li>
  <li>suppliers offer extended terms</li>
</ul>

<p>But negative working capital is not automatically healthy. A company can also have negative working capital because it is struggling to pay bills or because its obligations have changed.</p>

<p>The number needs to be interpreted alongside cash flow and the underlying business model.</p>

## How can you improve the cash conversion cycle?

<p>There are three main levers.</p>

### Improve DIO

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">If inventory is sitting too long, you can:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Reduce slow-moving stock</li>
  <li>Improve demand forecasting</li>
  <li>Order smaller quantities more frequently</li>
  <li>Remove low-performing products</li>
  <li>Improve inventory turnover</li>
</ul>

<p>The goal is to turn inventory into sales without carrying unnecessary stock.</p>

### Improve DSO

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">If customers are paying slowly, you can:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Invoice immediately</li>
  <li>Make payment terms explicit</li>
  <li>Automate payment reminders</li>
  <li>Offer convenient payment methods</li>
  <li>Review customers with repeated late payments</li>
  <li>Negotiate shorter payment terms for new contracts</li>
</ul>

<p>For many B2B companies, DSO is the most directly controllable part of CCC. A customer paying on day 30 instead of day 60 can materially change the company's cash position.</p>

### Improve DPO

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">If suppliers are being paid too quickly, you can:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Negotiate longer payment terms</li>
  <li>Align payment dates with customer collections</li>
  <li>Consolidate vendor payments</li>
  <li>Avoid paying invoices significantly before they are due</li>
</ul>

<p>The goal is not to pay suppliers late. It is to avoid giving up cash earlier than necessary.</p>

## Can a growing startup have a good CCC and still run out of cash?

<p>Yes.</p>

<p>CCC measures the operating cycle. It does not replace a cash flow forecast or <a href="/blog/runway-vs-burn-rate-for-founders" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">runway calculation</a>.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">A startup can have a healthy CCC and still face a cash shortage because of:</p>

<ul>
  <li>payroll growth</li>
  <li>large one-time purchases</li>
  <li>tax payments</li>
  <li>debt repayments</li>
  <li>capital expenditures</li>
  <li>fundraising delays</li>
  <li>unexpected expenses</li>
</ul>

<p>Likewise, a company can have a poor CCC but enough cash to operate comfortably.</p>

<p>CCC tells you how efficiently working capital moves. A cash flow forecast tells you whether you will have enough money to meet future obligations.</p>

<p>For startup operators, you need both.</p>

## How does CCC affect startup runway?

<p>CCC affects how much cash the business needs to support its operating plan, which can indirectly affect runway.</p>

<p>Suppose a startup has $500,000 in cash and expects to grow rapidly over the next year.</p>

<p>If customer collections slow while vendor payments remain unchanged, more cash becomes trapped in accounts receivable. The company may then need to fund that working capital gap from its existing bank balance. That can shorten runway.</p>

<p>This is why runway should not be modeled only from a simple monthly burn average. A forecast should also account for when cash actually moves.</p>

## What is a good cash conversion cycle for a SaaS company?

<p>There is no universal "good" CCC for SaaS because SaaS businesses usually have little or no inventory and often have billing models that differ substantially from product businesses.</p>

<p>For a SaaS company, DSO and customer prepayment behavior are usually more relevant than DIO.</p>

<p>An annual contract paid upfront can bring cash into the company before the service is delivered. A monthly contract with Net 60 terms creates a very different cash profile.</p>

<p>This is why founders should avoid comparing CCC mechanically across different business models. Instead, compare your metrics over time and against companies with similar operating models.</p>

## How should founders use CCC in a cash flow forecast?

<p>CCC is most useful when it becomes an operating management tool rather than a quarterly reporting metric.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Start with the historical numbers. Then ask:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Is DSO increasing?</li>
  <li>Is inventory building faster than revenue?</li>
  <li>Are supplier terms changing?</li>
  <li>Is growth consuming more working capital?</li>
  <li>What happens if collections are 15 days slower?</li>
  <li>How much additional cash would that require?</li>
</ul>

<p>This connects accounting metrics to actual cash planning.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">For example:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Current DSO = 40 days</li>
  <li>Scenario DSO = 55 days</li>
  <li>Increase = 15 days</li>
</ul>

<p>That 15-day change can represent a substantial amount of additional accounts receivable as revenue scales. The exact cash impact depends on sales volume and payment patterns, so the best approach is to model it directly in the forecast.</p>

## Worked example: how changing DSO changes cash needs

<p>Consider a B2B company generating:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Annual sales = $3,650,000</li>
  <li>Approximately $10,000 per day</li>
</ul>

<p>Now compare two collection scenarios.</p>

### Scenario A: 30-day DSO

<p><strong>$10,000 × 30 = $300,000</strong></p>

<p>Approximately $300,000 of sales would be represented by 30 days of receivables at this simplified run rate.</p>

### Scenario B: 60-day DSO

<p><strong>$10,000 × 60 = $600,000</strong></p>

<p>The difference is:</p>

<p><strong>$600,000 − $300,000 = $300,000</strong></p>

<p>So a 30-day increase in collection timing can create a $300,000 working capital requirement at this illustrative sales rate.</p>

<p>This is not an industry benchmark. It is a simplified example showing why collections can have a direct impact on cash requirements.</p>

<p>Revenue can look unchanged while the bank balance moves significantly.</p>

## When should a startup pay attention to the cash conversion cycle?

<p>Start paying attention when working capital can materially affect cash.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">That usually happens when you have:</p>

<ul>
  <li>physical inventory</li>
  <li>B2B payment terms</li>
  <li>significant accounts receivable</li>
  <li>large supplier balances</li>
  <li>rapid growth</li>
  <li>long implementation cycles</li>
  <li>large upfront procurement costs</li>
</ul>

<p>For an early SaaS startup that collects annual subscriptions upfront and carries almost no inventory, CCC may not be the most important operating metric.</p>

<p>For a hardware startup growing rapidly on Net 60 customer terms, it can become critical.</p>

<p>The metric should follow the business model.</p>

<p>The cash conversion cycle answers a simple but important question: how long does our cash stay tied up in the business before it comes back?</p>

<p><strong>CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO</strong></p>

<p>But the real value comes from understanding the three numbers underneath it.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Read the components:</p>

<ul>
  <li>If DSO is rising, customers are taking longer to pay.</li>
  <li>If DIO is rising, more cash is sitting in inventory.</li>
  <li>If DPO is falling, cash is leaving the business sooner.</li>
</ul>
