## The Problem with "Everything's Fine on Paper"

<p id="blog-article-lead" className="text-lg leading-relaxed text-muted-foreground">Here's a scenario we've all seen:</p>

<p>Your business is growing. Sales are up 30% year-over-year. Your customers are profitable. Everything looks great on the income statement.</p>

<p>But then you check your bank account and wonder how the hell you're supposed to <a href="/blog/will-i-make-payroll" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">pay this month's payroll</a>.</p>

<p><strong>This isn't a cash flow problem. It's a timing problem.</strong></p>

<p>You probably pay your suppliers in 30 days. Your customers pay you in 45. That 15-day gap adds up when you have to fund inventory, cover payroll, handle unexpected repairs, or invest in new equipment.</p>

<p>The <strong>cash conversion cycle (CCC)</strong> measures exactly this: how long it takes your cash to complete the loop from paying suppliers → holding inventory → getting paid by customers → cash in the bank.</p>

<p>Think of it as your business's <strong>working capital heartbeat</strong>.</p>

## The CCC Formula (Yes, It's Simple)

<p><strong>CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO</strong></p>

<p>Or spelled out:</p>

<p><strong>Cash Conversion Cycle = Days Inventory Outstanding + Days Sales Outstanding − Days Payable Outstanding</strong></p>

<p>Where:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>DIO</strong> = How many days it takes to sell your inventory</li>
  <li><strong>DSO</strong> = How many days it takes to collect from customers</li>
  <li><strong>DPO</strong> = How many days you get to wait before paying suppliers</li>
</ul>

<p>A lower CCC means your cash moves faster through the system. But here's what most people get wrong: <strong>CCC is an average, not a crystal ball.</strong></p>

<p>A 45-day CCC doesn't mean "my cash comes back in exactly 45 days." It means on average, your business ties up cash for 45 days before it returns.</p>

<p>Your actual bank balance on any given week depends on the specific dates your customers pay you and you pay your vendors. That's why I always pair CCC analysis with a <a href="/blog/what-is-a-13-week-cash-flow-forecast" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline"><strong>13-week cash flow forecast</strong></a>.</p>

### What This Means

<p>The manufacturer has a <strong>45-day cash conversion cycle</strong>. Here's how to read that:</p>

<ul>
  <li>They keep inventory for 50 days before selling</li>
  <li>Customers take 40 days to pay</li>
  <li>But they can keep their suppliers' money for 45 days</li>
  <li>Net result: cash is tied up for 45 days</li>
</ul>

<p>So in this business, <strong>supplier financing is offsetting about half of their operating cycle</strong>. That's actually pretty decent.</p>

<p>Same CCC number. Completely different problems.</p>

<p><strong>The takeaway:</strong> Don't just obsess over the total CCC. Look at which component is dragging you down, because the fix might be very different for each.</p>

## DIO vs DSO vs DPO: The Breakdown

<p>Let's go deeper into each component.</p>

### DIO: Days Inventory Outstanding

<p><strong>Formula:</strong></p>

<p>DIO = Average Inventory ÷ COGS × Days in Period</p>

<p>This tells you how long, on average, inventory sits before it sells.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> High DIO means money tied up in unsold stock, whether that's excess purchasing, weak demand forecasts, or slow-moving products. Low DIO is good, but don't understock to cut it; you'll lose sales.</p>

<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> A super-low DIO could mean you're not carrying enough stock, which leads to angry customers and lost revenue. Balance is key.</p>

### DSO: Days Sales Outstanding

<p><strong>Formula:</strong></p>

<p>DSO = Average Trade A/R ÷ Net Credit Sales × Days in Period</p>

<p>This measures how long it takes customers to pay you.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> A high DSO means your cash is working for someone else. Every day customers delay payment, that's a day longer your cash is stuck in receivables instead of being available for payroll, vendors, or growth.</p>

<p><strong>Pro tip:</strong> Use <em>net credit sales</em> (not total sales) in the denominator if you have cash and credit sales. If all your sales are credit, just use total sales as an approximation.</p>

### DPO: Days Payable Outstanding

<p><strong>Formula:</strong></p>

<p>DPO = Average Trade A/P ÷ COGS or Purchases × Days in Period</p>

<p>This tells you how long it takes to pay your suppliers.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> This is your <strong>supplier financing</strong>. You get the goods, sell them, get paid by customers, and then pay suppliers. If you pay vendors in 45 days, your suppliers are financing you for 45 days.</p>

<p><strong>Important:</strong> Don't just dump all your liabilities into A/P. Only include <strong>trade accounts payable</strong> (money owed to suppliers for inventory/purchases). Payroll, taxes, and loans are different buckets.</p>

<p><strong>The catch:</strong> You can't ethically or practically pay invoices 90 days late when your contract says 30. Negotiate longer terms, don't break relationships.</p>

## CCC vs. Operating Cycle: What's the Difference?

<p>People mix these up. Here's the distinction:</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="The operating cycle is gross time from buying inventory to collecting cash; the cash conversion cycle nets out supplier credit."
  columns={['Metric', 'Formula', 'What It Captures']}
  rows={[
    [<strong>Operating Cycle</strong>, 'DIO + DSO', 'Time from buying inventory to collecting cash'],
    [<strong>Cash Conversion Cycle</strong>, 'DIO + DSO − DPO', <><strong>Net</strong> time cash is tied up (includes supplier credit)</>],
  ]}
/>

<p>Think of it as <strong>gross vs net</strong>:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Operating cycle</strong> = your business's full operating loop (gross)</li>
  <li><strong>CCC</strong> = operating cycle minus the supplier credit you're "renting" (net)</li>
</ul>

<p>If your DPO is 45 days and your operating cycle is 90 days, you get a 45-day CCC. The supplier is effectively funding about half your working capital needs.</p>

## What Is a "Good" CCC?

<p>Honestly? <strong>There's no universal answer.</strong></p>

<p>A grocery store that sells fresh produce in 2 days has a totally different working capital model than a manufacturer producing complex equipment that sits in inventory for 90 days.</p>

<p>Instead of fixating on some magic number, ask:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Trend:</strong> Is our CCC improving or getting worse over time?</li>
  <li><strong>Comparables:</strong> How do we stack up against truly similar businesses?</li>
  <li><strong>Component diagnosis:</strong> If CCC is high, which part is driving it?</li>
  <li><strong>Sustainability:</strong> Are we improving terms responsibly, or just stretching suppliers?</li>
  <li><strong>Cash impact:</strong> How does this affect our actual bank balance?</li>
</ol>

<p>For most founders: Compare to your own historical numbers first. If your CCC goes from 30 to 50 days, investigate, regardless of whether 30 or 50 "should" be acceptable in isolation.</p>

## Negative CCC: Friend or Foe?

<p>A negative CCC occurs when <strong>DPO &gt; (DIO + DSO)</strong>. In plain English: your customers are paying you before you need to pay your suppliers.</p>

<p><strong>Good negative CCC:</strong> Customers prepay, or you negotiate favorable terms. <strong>Bad negative CCC:</strong> You're paying suppliers late.</p>

<p>For example:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Company X:</strong> DIO = 20 days, DSO = 5 days, DPO = 40 days → CCC = −15 days</li>
  <li>They collect from customers quickly and let suppliers wait 40 days</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Red flag:</strong> If DPO is high because <em>you're overdue to your suppliers</em>, that's not a competitive advantage. That's a relationship and cash flow crisis waiting to happen.</p>

<p>A negative CCC can look pretty on paper while hiding serious working capital stress. Always check <em>why</em> you're in negative territory.</p>

## How to Actually Improve Your CCC

<p>You have three levers to pull. Here's where to start depending on what's broken.</p>

### 1. Reduce DSO: Get Paid Faster (Low-Hanging Fruit)

<p>Collections are usually the fastest, cheapest way to improve cash.</p>

<p><strong>Start with your A/R aging:</strong></p>

<BlogTable
  caption="An A/R aging summary: what customers owe you, grouped by how old each invoice is."
  columns={['Bucket', 'Amount']}
  rows={[
    ['0–30 days', '$X'],
    ['31–60 days', '$Y'],
    ['61+ days', '$Z'],
  ]}
  numeric={[1]}
/>

<p><strong>Find out why:</strong> Late invoicing? Billing errors? Disputed charges? Missing PO numbers?</p>

<p><strong>Then take action:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>Invoice immediately after delivery or milestone completion</li>
  <li>Confirm billing contacts and PO requirements upfront</li>
  <li>Offer multiple payment options (ACH, card, wire, invoice terms)</li>
  <li>Automate payment reminders before and after due dates</li>
  <li>Review overdue accounts <strong>weekly</strong>, not quarterly</li>
  <li>For large B2B clients, discuss term reduction during quarterly reviews</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> A $10,000/day sales business where DSO goes from 30 to 45 days = ~$150,000 more cash tied up.</p>

### 2. Reduce DIO: Stop Overstocking

<p>Excess inventory is expensive: both the money you spent and the ongoing carrying costs.</p>

<p><strong>Diagnose first:</strong></p>

<BlogTable
  caption="Four inventory checks to run before cutting stock."
  columns={['Metric', 'What to check']}
  rows={[
    ['Turnover', 'Are some items barely selling?'],
    ['Age', 'Do we have 6+ month old stock?'],
    ['Value', 'How much is this SKU worth?'],
    ['Margin', 'Are we overbuying low-margin items?'],
  ]}
/>

<p><strong>Then fix:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>Tighten reorder points to actual demand, not "gut feel"</li>
  <li>Stop buying safety stock that's never been needed</li>
  <li>Clear obsolete inventory (discount, donate, liquidate)</li>
  <li>Align purchasing with confirmed demand, especially in volatile markets</li>
  <li>Review supplier lead times; JIT makes sense for fast movers</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Balance:</strong> Don't understock to chase low DIO. Stockouts have real costs: lost sales, customer churn, and opportunity costs.</p>

### 3. Improve DPO: Use Supplier Financing (Carefully)

<p>Supplier credit is like <strong>free money</strong>. But you can't abuse it.</p>

<p><strong>Smart DPO improvement:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>Negotiate longer terms (e.g., 30 → 45 → 60 days)</li>
  <li>Bundle purchases to justify longer payment cycles</li>
  <li>Take early-pay discounts when you have the cash. It lowers DPO, but a good discount can beat the extra days.</li>
  <li>Stagger vendor payments to match collection dates</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>What NOT to do:</strong> Systematically pay everyone 2x as late as your contract allows. That will kill relationships, and suppliers will either stop shipping or start billing you penalties.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> Switching from Net 30 to Net 60 on your top 5 suppliers might give you meaningful cash relief without burning bridges.</p>

## CCC vs. Cash Flow Forecast: Two Different Tools

<p>This is critical for founders.</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="What the cash conversion cycle and a 13-week cash flow forecast each tell you, and what they don't."
  columns={['Tool', 'What It Does', "What It Doesn't Tell You"]}
  rows={[
    [<strong>CCC</strong>, 'Average working capital timing over a period', 'Exact dates of cash inflows/outflows'],
    [<strong>13-Week Forecast</strong>, 'Week-by-week cash in and out', 'Long-term average efficiency'],
  ]}
/>

<p><strong>Why you need both:</strong></p>

<p>CCC tells you "on average, my cash turns over every 45 days." A cash forecast tells you "<a href="/blog/zero-cash-date-for-founders" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">I'll run out of cash in Week 4</a>."</p>

<p>Example:</p>

<ul>
  <li>You have a 45-day CCC</li>
  <li>Your biggest customer pays $150,000 in Week 5 instead of Week 3</li>
  <li>You go into Week 4 with $25,000 in the bank</li>
  <li>You need to pay $80,000 payroll in Week 4</li>
  <li>Your bank balance hits <strong>negative $55,000</strong> in Week 4</li>
</ul>

<p>Same CCC. Different cash reality because of <strong>timing</strong>.</p>

<p>This is why I always <a href="/blog/how-to-build-a-13-week-cash-flow-forecast-in-excel" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">build a <strong>13-week cash flow forecast</strong></a> after diagnosing CCC issues.</p>

## CCC for SaaS, Agencies, and Non-Inventory Businesses

<p>The traditional CCC formula was built for <strong>inventory businesses</strong>. It doesn't always work for others.</p>

### SaaS (Software as a Service)

<p>Most SaaS has no physical inventory, so DIO is zero or irrelevant.</p>

<p><strong>But wait, what about <a href="/blog/arr-vs-cash-for-founders" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">annual subscriptions</a>?</strong></p>

<p>A customer pays $12,000 upfront for a 12-month subscription, but the company recognizes $1,000 in revenue per month. The upfront cash helps, but the traditional CCC formula can be misleading.</p>

<p><strong>Better metrics for SaaS:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>Days to subscription collection (from contract to first payment)</li>
  <li>Average accounts receivable</li>
  <li>Deferred revenue</li>
  <li><a href="/blog/runway-vs-burn-rate-for-founders" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Cash runway</a></li>
  <li>Monthly burn rate</li>
</ul>

### Agencies and Service Businesses

<p><a href="/blog/cash-flow-forecasting-for-agencies" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Agencies</a> may not have inventory at all. Their main costs are payroll, contractors, and software subscriptions.</p>

<p><strong>What to track instead:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>DSO (still matters: when clients pay you)</li>
  <li>Days to first major cost payment (payroll, hosting, software)</li>
  <li>Contract milestone timing</li>
  <li>Accounts receivable</li>
  <li>Cash runway vs. obligations</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> You complete work on March 15, invoice the client on April 5, and they pay on May 5. Meanwhile, you pay payroll on March 31. That's a cash gap, not a CCC gap.</p>

### Ecommerce

<p>The traditional CCC model works well for inventory-based ecommerce.</p>

<p>You buy inventory → hold it → sell it → get paid → pay suppliers.</p>

<p>The same three metrics (DIO, DSO, DPO) are highly relevant.</p>

## The Bottom Line

<p>The cash conversion cycle gives you a practical lens into <strong>working capital efficiency</strong>.</p>

<p>You can calculate it in minutes using your balance sheet and income statement. You can spot whether inventory, collections, or supplier terms are your biggest cash drag.</p>

<p>But here's what I can't overstate: <strong>CCC is not your crystal ball.</strong></p>

<p>It tells you the average timing of cash tied up in operations. It doesn't tell you the exact week you'll run out of cash.</p>

<p><strong>For actual cash planning:</strong> Build a 13-week cash flow forecast.</p>

<p><strong>For performance tracking:</strong> Monitor CCC trends quarter over quarter.</p>

<p><strong>For strategy:</strong> Use CCC analysis to decide which working capital levers to pull first, and whether they're actually improving cash or just moving the problem around.</p>

<p>Because in the end, it's not about optimizing an accounting formula. It's about <strong>knowing when your cash will run out</strong> and having a plan before that day hits.</p>
