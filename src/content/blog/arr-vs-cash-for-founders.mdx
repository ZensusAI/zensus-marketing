<p id="blog-article-lead" className="text-lg leading-relaxed text-muted-foreground">I'll tell you something nobody puts in their investor decks:</p>

<p><strong>ARR and cash are not the same thing.</strong></p>

<p>Don't roll your eyes. Hear me out.</p>

<p>When you're scaling a SaaS business, you want to hear that your Annual Recurring Revenue is growing. Nothing sounds better than seeing that number climb from $100K to $500K to $2M. Your investors are nodding and smiling. Your pitch deck charts look killer.</p>

<p>But then you check your bank account.</p>

<p><strong>And it's not moving.</strong></p>

<p>Maybe it's flat. Maybe it's bleeding out faster than it should be.</p>

<p>You've got a growing pipeline, a solid retention rate, maybe even strong net revenue retention... and yet when it comes time to pay the team, cover expenses, or just survive the next quarter, the money isn't there.</p>

<p><strong>This is not a fantasy.</strong> This is the daily reality for most SaaS founders I've worked with.</p>

## Why ARR Doesn't Predict Your Next Payroll

<p>Let's break down why this happens.</p>

<p>ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) measures the annualized value of your active subscriptions at a point in time. Your bank balance reflects money actually collected, minus money actually spent.</p>

<p>These are three different measurements:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>ARR:</strong> how much recurring subscription value your active customers represent on an annualized basis</li>
  <li><strong>Cash flow:</strong> money moving into and out of your business over time</li>
  <li><strong>Bank balance:</strong> what's actually sitting in your account right now</li>
</ol>

<p>A $120,000 subscription can increase ARR by $120,000 without generating $120,000 of immediate cash. And understanding why matters, because this difference determines whether you can <a href="/blog/will-i-make-payroll" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">make payroll</a> next month.</p>

<p>Most founders think they know what ARR means. But when I break this down for them, they realize their metric definitions are way off.</p>

<p>Here's the reality:</p>

<p>A sixth metric, <strong>bookings</strong>, records customer commitments before they're active or invoiced. Like ARR, it's a non-GAAP metric, and it varies by company.</p>

<p><strong>A sale does not necessarily mean an invoice. An invoice does not necessarily mean payment. And payment does not necessarily mean the company has earned all the revenue.</strong></p>

<p>These metrics are connected, but they're not interchangeable. And when you mix them up, your financial decisions become dangerous.</p>

## Follow One $120,000 Contract From Promise to Payday

<p>I want to show you exactly what happens to a SaaS contract, from the moment it's signed to when the revenue is actually recognized.</p>

<p>Imagine your company signs a customer for a one-year subscription:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Annual price: <strong>$120,000</strong></li>
  <li>Service period: January 1 through December 31</li>
  <li>Billing: Annual, upfront</li>
  <li>Invoice date: January 1</li>
  <li>Payment date: January 1</li>
</ul>

<p>The service gets delivered evenly over 12 months. No surprises: no discounts, no credits, no early churning, no taxes or fees for this example.</p>

### Here's the journey:

<p><strong>Step 1: ARR Increases by $120,000</strong></p>

<p>On January 1, when the subscription becomes active, you add $120,000 to your ARR. Your MRR (monthly recurring revenue) equivalent is $10,000.</p>

<p>But hear me here: <strong>This doesn't mean $120,000 will be deposited every month.</strong> It's the annualized value of that active subscription. It's a point-in-time metric.</p>

<p><strong>Step 2: You Issue a $120,000 Invoice</strong></p>

<p>Same day. January 1. The customer receives an invoice for the full annual amount.</p>

<p>This isn't cash yet. This is a bill. An invoice is not a bank deposit.</p>

<p><strong>Step 3: The Customer Pays $120,000</strong></p>

<p>The payment settles on January 1. The money appears in your bank account.</p>

<p>That's when the cash movement actually happens. January cash collections: <strong>$120,000</strong>.</p>

<p>Now, you've committed to providing software access for 12 months. The cash has arrived. Twelve months of service still remain.</p>

<p><strong>Step 4: Revenue Is Recognized Over Time</strong></p>

<p>This is where accounting gets real. Under <a href="https://storage.fasb.org/ASU%202014-09_Section%20A.pdf" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">standard accounting rules</a>, you recognize revenue as services are delivered, not when money arrives.</p>

<p>If the service is delivered evenly, you recognize <strong>$10,000 per month</strong> ($120,000 ÷ 12).</p>

<p>By the end of January, you've recognized $10,000 in revenue. The remaining $110,000 becomes <strong>deferred revenue</strong>: it's money you've collected but haven't earned yet.</p>

<p><strong>Step 5: The Full Picture</strong></p>

<p>Here's what that same $120,000 contract looks like across the year:</p>

  caption="One $120,000 annual contract paid upfront on January 1: ARR stays flat, cash arrives once, and revenue is recognized $10,000 a month."
  columns={['Metric', 'January', 'February', 'December']}

    ['Active ARR', '$120,000', '$120,000', '$120,000'],

    ['Cash collected', '$120,000', '$0', '$0'],

<p>See the pattern?</p>

<p><strong>First, ARR stays constant.</strong> Once the contract is active, it stays on the books until the end. It doesn't add $120,000 every month. That would be wrong.</p>

<p><strong>Second, cash arrives all at once.</strong> No payment arrives from this contract after January.</p>

<p><strong>Third, revenue keeps flowing even without payments.</strong> That's the core of subscription accounting.</p>

<p>But here's what none of this tells you: <strong>What's left in the bank at the end of the month, after payroll and expenses?</strong></p>

<p>To answer that question, you need to think about outflows, not just inflows.</p>

<p>Same customer. Same $120,000 annual contract. But now they're billed <strong>monthly</strong>, not upfront.</p>

<p>The subscription becomes active on January 1, so ARR jumps by $120,000. You invoice $10,000 that month. But the customer doesn't pay until... well, not this month.</p>

<p>Meanwhile, your business is running. Payroll. Infrastructure. Vendors. All consuming cash.</p>

<p><strong>The problem:</strong> The ARR increase hasn't turned into cash receipts yet. Meanwhile, you've been burning cash to keep the business running.</p>

<p>This is why founders ask: <strong>"We added $500K in ARR this quarter. Why is our bank account not growing?"</strong></p>

<p>And this is why I always remind them: <strong>"How much ARR did we add" is not the same question as "Can we make payroll next week?"</strong></p>

## The Five Dates That Control Your Cash Reality

<p>When you think about SaaS contracts, there are five key dates you need to track. I don't know how many of you actually do this, but you should:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Subscription activation:</strong> When does this contract start contributing to active ARR?</li>
  <li><strong>Invoice issuance:</strong> When does the customer get billed?</li>
  <li><strong>Payment settlement:</strong> When does the money actually land in your bank?</li>
  <li><strong>Revenue recognition:</strong> When does the company recognize this as earned revenue?</li>
  <li><strong>Expense payments:</strong> When does the company need to pay payroll and vendors?</li>
</ol>

<p>Your contract signing date is often a sixth event that happens before the first one.</p>

<p>When these dates don't line up, ARR and cash can move in opposite directions.</p>

<p>A customer might sign in January, start service in February, get an invoice in March, and pay in April.</p>

<p>If your forecasting tool treats all these events as happening on the same day, it's not telling you the truth about your liquidity.</p>

<p><strong>Customer A</strong> pays annually upfront. <strong>Customer B</strong> pays monthly.</p>

<p>Both subscriptions have the same total cash over the year. Both represent $12,000 in ARR.</p>

<p>But their cash timing is completely different.</p>

<p><strong>Annual billing upfront?</strong> You get that cash right now. That can help you cover payroll, pay for the engineers building the new feature, or just breathe easier this quarter.</p>

<p><strong>Monthly billing?</strong> You get money spread across 12 months. Smaller, steady streams. Less risk of hitting a cliff if one payment bounces.</p>

<p>So which is better?</p>

<p><strong>For cash timing in the near term?</strong> Annual billing usually wins. You have more cash available to work with early in the contract.</p>

<p><strong>But annual billing doesn't automatically:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>Increase your ARR (if both customers are on equivalent plans)</li>
  <li>Improve your profitability (costs are the same)</li>
  <li>Eliminate churn or renewal risk</li>
  <li>Extend your runway past the contract year</li>
</ul>

<p>A company relying heavily on annual renewals might have a fat cash balance right now. But when that payment is due again, what if the customer churns? What if they go bankrupt? What if the renewal gets delayed?</p>

<p><strong>Annual prepayment accelerates cash collection. It doesn't create magic money.</strong></p>

<p>It changes when you have it, not how much you'll ever make.</p>

## What Does Deferred Revenue Actually Mean?

<p>Every founder knows what "deferred revenue" sounds like in investor deck language. The reality is messier.</p>

<p>Deferred revenue (or contract liability in accounting terms) happens when you've collected (or are already owed) cash you haven't earned yet.</p>

<p>Let's say a customer pays $120,000 upfront for a year of service. You record a cash inflow. But you also have an obligation: you still need to deliver that service over the remaining months.</p>

<p>In accounting terms, this creates a <strong>contract liability</strong>. As you deliver the service month by month, you reduce the liability and recognize revenue.</p>

<p>Here's the part no one tells you:</p>

<p><strong>An unpaid invoice that's already due can also create deferred revenue.</strong></p>

<p>Imagine a customer who agrees to pay $50,000 in November for a service they'll receive in December. You send the invoice in November, but they don't pay until... they don't pay until next year.</p>

<p>Until you deliver December's service, you have a contract liability. You have <strong>no cash</strong>, but you have a deferred revenue obligation.</p>

<p><strong>Deferred revenue represents future service you owe. It is not cash available to spend.</strong></p>

## Why Two $1M ARR Companies Can Have Different Runway

<p>Let me set up a scenario.</p>

<p><strong>Company A:</strong> 100 customers on $10,000 annual contracts. They pay upfront.</p>

<p><strong>Company B:</strong> Also 100 customers, same $10,000 annual contracts. They pay monthly.</p>

<p>Both have <strong>$1 million ARR</strong>.</p>

<p>Let's assume their monthly cash expenses are identical: $120,000 per month for payroll, hosting, and everything else.</p>

<p><strong>Early in the year:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>Company A has a lot more cash. Their renewals just hit.</li>
  <li>Company B is collecting slowly. Cash is tight.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Six months later:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>Company A's initial wave is done. No more customer cash arrives until renewal, and its balance falls $120,000 every month.</li>
  <li>Company B's monthly collections cover part of its costs, so its balance falls more slowly. But it never had the early cushion.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Bottom line:</strong> The timing of when payments arrive creates dramatically different cash positions.</p>

<p>And it's not just about who has more ARR at the end of the year. It's about <strong>when they need the cash</strong>.</p>

<p>A company might finish a month with $100,000 in the bank, but need $150,000 by Thursday for payroll and rent.</p>

<p><strong>A monthly revenue dashboard doesn't tell you that.</strong></p>

<p>A dated cash flow forecast does.</p>

## Here's How to Actually Forecast Your Cash

<p>Forget about "How much ARR will we add next quarter?" and ask yourself:</p>

<p><strong>"What's our cash balance going to look like in <a href="/blog/what-is-a-13-week-cash-flow-forecast" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">13 weeks</a>, and what could break it?"</strong></p>

<p>Here's a practical approach that works for real founders:</p>

### Step 1: Start With What's Actually in the Bank

<p>Pull your latest bank statement. Get the reconciled balance. Separate what you can actually spend now from what's pending or restricted.</p>

<p><strong>This is your starting point.</strong> Not ARR. Not projected revenue. The cash you have today.</p>

### Step 2: Map When Customers Actually Pay

<p>For each meaningful customer, note these dates:</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="The four fields to track for each meaningful customer."
  columns={['Field', 'What it is']}
  rows={[
    ['Next invoice amount', 'How much are they billed this cycle?'],
    ['Invoice date', 'When was the bill sent?'],
    ['Payment due date', 'When must they pay?'],
    ['Expected settlement date', 'When will the money land in your account?'],
  ]}
/>

<p>Track <strong>expected settlement date</strong>, not invoice date.</p>

<p>Your customer with Net-30 terms doesn't always pay in 30 days. Maybe they pay in 42 days. Maybe they pay late, or don't pay at all.</p>

<p>Use your payment history. Update it as new information arrives.</p>

### Step 3: Don't Forget Outflows

<p>Forecast when you'll pay:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Payroll and contractor payments</li>
  <li>Cloud infrastructure bills</li>
  <li>Software vendors</li>
  <li>Taxes and obligations</li>
  <li>Rent and operating costs</li>
  <li>Debt payments</li>
  <li>Customer refunds or credits</li>
</ul>

<p>If your forecast only includes incoming cash and ignores expenses, it's lying to you.</p>

### Step 4: Build the Weekly Bridge

<p>Calculate <a href="/blog/how-to-build-a-13-week-cash-flow-forecast-in-excel" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">week by week</a>:</p>

<p><strong>Ending cash = Beginning cash + Inflows − Outflows</strong></p>

<p>Here's a sketch of what that looks like:</p>

  caption="A four-week cash bridge from $200,000: week 3 is the low point at $130,000."

  numeric={[1, 2, 3, 4]}

/>

<p>The company starts with $200,000 and ends week 4 with $140,000.</p>

<p>But <strong>Week 3</strong> is where you should be nervous. Cash dips to $130,000.</p>

<p>If most of that week's $40,000 goes out on Monday and the $5,000 collection doesn't land until Friday, Monday is the real low point, not the week-end balance.</p>

<p><strong>A week's balance doesn't tell you about daily cash needs.</strong></p>

### Step 5: Stress-Test the Forecast

<p>What if:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Your largest customer pays 30 days late?</li>
  <li>A $250,000 enterprise renewal slips into the next quarter?</li>
  <li>Three mid-market customers downgrade?</li>
  <li>Payroll goes up 15% due to hiring?</li>
  <li>That enterprise contract never activates?</li>
</ul>

<p>Adjust one variable at a time. Recalculate. See how the minimum cash balance shifts.</p>

<p>Compare the result against your <a href="/blog/cash-flow-early-warning-system" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">cash floor</a>: whatever cash you absolutely need to keep operating.</p>

<p>That's how you answer: <strong><a href="/blog/zero-cash-date-for-founders" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">When will we run out of cash?</a></strong></p>

## The Founder's Diagnostic: ARR Is Up, So Why Isn't Cash?

<p>Let's say ARR grew this month, but your bank balance went down. Here's what to check:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Are those new contracts actually active? Or are they future-start bookings that shouldn't count yet?</li>
  <li>Have you invoiced all your active customers? Any billing configuration gaps?</li>
  <li>How much has been invoiced but remains unpaid? (Accounts Receivable)</li>
  <li>Are payments 30, 60, 90 days overdue?</li>
  <li>Are your annual renewals due next quarter?</li>
  <li>Have customers started payments that haven't cleared yet?</li>
  <li>Have you issued credits or processed refunds?</li>
  <li>Is your payroll, vendor spending, or taxes growing faster than collections?</li>
  <li>Are you missing key outflows in your accounting?</li>
  <li>Do your bank statement, payment processor, and accounting books match on dates?</li>
</ul>

<p>Don't look for a magic explanation. Look for <strong>where in the sequence is the break</strong>.</p>

<p><strong>Subscription → Invoice → Payment → Bank cash → Operating expenses</strong></p>

<p>Each event has an amount and a date. Once you map them all, the gap between ARR growth and cash flow becomes obvious.</p>

## The Bottom Line

<p>ARR and cash are not enemies. They're different metrics answering different questions.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>ARR</strong> tells you how big your recurring business is growing.</li>
  <li><strong>Cash flow</strong> tells you whether you're spending more than you're collecting.</li>
  <li><strong>Bank balance</strong> shows what you have right now.</li>
</ul>

<p>Your bank account can stay flat even as ARR grows, because new contracts take time to convert into payments. Your balance can drop even as ARR climbs, because expenses eat through collections faster than payments arrive.</p>

<p>A company can collect $120,000 upfront and have plenty of cash for now, but burn through it all in two months. Or it can add $120,000 in ARR while collecting nothing from the new customer that month.</p>

<p>The question isn't "Is our ARR growing?"</p>

<p><strong>ARR tells you how much recurring business you've built. A dated cash flow forecast tells you whether you can afford the decisions you're about to make.</strong></p>
