<p id="blog-article-lead" className="text-lg leading-relaxed text-muted-foreground">Overhead costs are the indirect expenses a business incurs to support its operations. Think of them as the bills you pay just to keep the doors open: office rent, insurance, administrative salaries, and accounting software. These are costs that can't be traced directly to producing a particular product or delivering a specific service.</p>

Unlike direct costs, overhead generally supports the business as a whole. It's the cost of having a business at all, not the cost of making a sale.

Understanding overhead matters because these expenses affect more than just profitability. They also create cash obligations that must be paid even when customers haven't settled their invoices.

Imagine running a marketing agency with $20,000 in monthly overhead. Your business generates $80,000 in revenue, and your income statement shows a nice profit.

But what happens when your biggest client pays four weeks late?

Your rent is still due. Payroll still needs to clear. Those software subscriptions keep charging your account whether you have new work lined up or not.

That's the distinction business owners need to understand: overhead affects your profitability, but the timing of overhead payments affects your cash flow.

This guide explains what overhead costs are, how to calculate them, and how to forecast their impact on your business.

## What Is Overhead in Business?

Overhead refers to the indirect costs required to operate a business. These expenses support all your day-to-day operations but can't be conveniently or economically assigned to a specific product, project, or customer.

Every business needs resources beyond those directly involved in delivering its products or services.

A consulting agency needs accounting software, insurance, and administrative support to function. A retailer needs store facilities, utilities, and management. A SaaS company incurs corporate administration, legal fees, and other shared operating costs.

These are all common examples of overhead.

### Direct Costs vs. Overhead Costs

The distinction comes down to how an expense relates to the product or service being delivered.

Consider a marketing agency producing a website for a client.

The developer working directly on the project may represent a direct labor cost.

The accountant preparing the agency's financial statements represents an indirect administrative cost.

Both are absolutely necessary. However, they serve different accounting and management purposes.

**An important distinction:** Not every direct cost changes immediately with sales, and not every overhead expense is fixed. Direct versus indirect describes cost attribution, while fixed versus variable describes cost behavior.

### Overhead Examples by Business Type

**SaaS companies**

Common administrative overhead includes corporate software subscriptions, legal fees, finance personnel, and general management expenses.

Cloud computing costs directly attributable to delivering a subscription service may instead be classified as cost of revenue.

**Marketing agencies**

Typical overhead includes office rent, administrative staff, insurance, and shared management systems.

Freelancers hired specifically for a client engagement generally represent direct project costs.

**Retail businesses**

Store management, corporate administration, and certain occupancy expenses may be overhead.

Merchandise purchased for resale is inventory and normally enters cost of goods sold when sold, rather than being treated as ordinary administrative overhead.

**Manufacturing companies**

Factory supervision, production-facility depreciation, and indirect production supplies are examples of manufacturing overhead.

These costs may be allocated to inventory and ultimately recognized in cost of goods sold.

The classification matters because it affects gross margin, operating profitability, and pricing decisions.

## The Three Types of Overhead Costs

Overhead can be categorized based on how expenses respond to changes in business activity.

### 1. Fixed Overhead Costs

Fixed overhead expenses remain relatively constant within a relevant range of activity.

Examples include:

- Monthly office rent
- Salaried administrative personnel
- Fixed software subscriptions
- Business insurance premiums
- Office equipment leases

Imagine an agency paying $5,000 in monthly rent.

Whether it serves five clients or fifteen, the rent generally remains $5,000 until the lease changes or the company requires additional space.

Fixed overhead creates predictable financial commitments.

However, predictable doesn't mean harmless. These obligations can become difficult to meet when revenue collections slow down.

### 2. Variable Overhead Costs

Variable overhead expenses change with an underlying activity driver.

Examples might include:

- Usage-based administrative software charges
- Transaction-based administrative fees
- Certain indirect supplies
- Usage-sensitive facility expenses

Suppose a company pays $100 monthly for an internal software platform plus $5 for every active employee.

As headcount increases, the usage-based portion of the expense rises.

Variable overhead becomes particularly important when a business scales because additional activity can generate additional indirect costs.

However, the relevant driver may be employees, transactions, equipment hours, or another activity measure, not necessarily revenue.

### 3. Semi-Variable Overhead Costs

Semi-variable, or mixed, overhead contains both fixed and variable components.

Consider a telecommunications contract:

- Fixed monthly charge: $100
- Usage charge: $0.50 per additional unit
- Monthly usage: 400 billable units

The expense would be:

**Total cost = $100 + (400 × $0.50) = $300**

The company owes $100 regardless of usage, but the remaining cost changes with activity.

Understanding this distinction helps business owners forecast expenses more accurately.

## How to Calculate Overhead Costs

Calculating overhead starts with identifying indirect expenses over a consistent accounting period.

### Step 1: Identify Your Indirect Expenses

Review your general ledger, income statement, and supporting transactions.

Separate expenses attributable to specific products or services from those supporting the business as a whole.

For accurate financial reporting, use the appropriate accounting classifications rather than relying exclusively on bank statement descriptions.

A cash payment and an accounting expense are not always recognized in the same period.

### Step 2: Calculate Total Overhead

Use the following formula:

**Total overhead = Sum of all relevant indirect costs for the period**

Consider a consulting agency with these monthly expenses:

  columns={['Overhead expense', 'Monthly amount']}
  rows={[

    ['Office supplies', '$400'],

    [<strong>Total monthly overhead</strong>, <strong>$13,450</strong>],
  ]}
  numeric={[1]}
/>

The business incurs $13,450 in monthly overhead under these assumptions.

### Step 3: Calculate Your Overhead Ratio

An overhead-to-revenue ratio measures indirect expenses relative to revenue.

**Overhead ratio = (Total overhead ÷ Total revenue) × 100**

Suppose the consulting agency earns $80,000 in revenue during the same month.

Overhead ratio = ($13,450 ÷ $80,000) × 100

Overhead ratio = 16.81%

This means overhead represents approximately 16.8% of the agency's monthly revenue.

The ratio can help track changes in cost structure over time.

However, it does not tell you whether the business is profitable on its own. You also need to account for direct expenses, other operating expenses, financing costs, and taxes.

### Step 4: Calculate an Overhead Allocation Rate

An overhead allocation rate assigns indirect costs to a product, service, or project using an appropriate allocation base.

**Overhead allocation rate = Total overhead to allocate ÷ Total allocation-base units**

Common allocation bases include:

- Direct labor hours
- Direct labor costs
- Machine hours
- Square footage
- Relevant transaction or activity volumes

For example, suppose a different consulting firm has:

- Budgeted monthly overhead: $12,000
- Budgeted monthly direct labor hours: 600

The overhead allocation rate is:

$12,000 ÷ 600 hours = $20 per direct labor hour

A project requiring 40 direct labor hours receives:

40 × $20 = $800 in allocated overhead

This allows management to estimate a more complete project cost.

### Overhead Ratio vs. Overhead Allocation Rate

These metrics answer different questions.

  rows={[
    ['Overhead-to-revenue ratio', 'Overhead ÷ Revenue × 100', 'Evaluate overall cost structure'],
    ['Overhead allocation rate', 'Overhead ÷ Allocation base', 'Assign indirect costs to products or projects'],

/>

The distinction is especially important when estimating profitability or setting customer prices.

## What Is a Good Overhead Percentage for a Small Business?

There is no universal healthy overhead percentage.

An appropriate overhead ratio depends on the industry, business model, growth stage, cost classifications, and operational strategy.

A consulting firm may have relatively high personnel-related expenses.

A SaaS company may incur substantial sales, engineering, and administrative spending while scaling.

A manufacturer may allocate significant factory overhead to inventory and cost of goods sold.

Comparing these businesses using a single percentage can be misleading.

### How Revenue Changes the Overhead Ratio

Consider a business with $12,000 in fixed monthly overhead.

As revenue increases, the same fixed overhead becomes a smaller percentage of revenue.

This illustrates operating leverage.

However, rising revenue may also require new employees, larger facilities, more software, or other investments.

The objective isn't necessarily to minimize overhead. It's to understand whether the spending contributes sufficient operational capacity and financial value.

### Questions to Ask When Evaluating Overhead

Rather than targeting an arbitrary percentage, ask:

- Is overhead growing faster than revenue?
- Which expenses are necessary to support operations?
- Are recurring costs justified by their value?
- How much overhead is committed versus discretionary?
- Can the business cover upcoming payments if collections are delayed?
- How would a new hiring decision change future cash requirements?

The final two questions are particularly important because a sustainable expense structure must work in both the income statement and the cash forecast.

## Overhead vs. Operating Expenses vs. COGS

Overhead, operating expenses, and cost of goods sold are related concepts, but they are not interchangeable.

<BlogTable
  caption="Overhead vs. operating expenses vs. cost of goods sold."
  columns={['Term', 'Meaning', 'Example']}
  rows={[
    ['Overhead', 'Indirect costs supporting business activities or production', 'Administrative salaries or factory supervision'],
    ['Operating expenses (OpEx)', 'Expenses incurred in operating the business, commonly including selling, general, and administrative expenses', 'Marketing, administration, corporate rent'],
    ['Cost of goods sold (COGS)', 'Costs associated with goods sold, including allocated manufacturing overhead where applicable', 'Materials, production labor, allocated factory costs'],
  ]}
/>

Administrative overhead is commonly included in operating expenses.

Manufacturing overhead, however, may be allocated to inventory and recognized in COGS as the goods are sold.

That distinction is important because not all overhead appears in the same section of an income statement.

### Example: A Small Bakery

A bakery incurs:

- Flour and sugar for bread production
- Baker wages directly involved in production
- Factory electricity
- Production supervisor salary
- Accounting software
- Office administrator salary

The ingredients and direct production labor are production costs.

Factory electricity and production supervision may be manufacturing overhead allocated to inventory.

Accounting software and general administrative salaries are ordinarily period operating expenses.

The accounting treatment affects when those costs appear in reported profit.

For more information about inventory and production cost treatment, see the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">IRS Tax Guide for Small Business</a>.

## How to Allocate Overhead to Products or Projects

Overhead allocation is useful when a company wants to determine the full cost of delivering a product or completing a client engagement.

Consider a consulting business estimating a new project.

Assume:

- Fully loaded direct labor cost: $50 per hour
- Overhead allocation rate: $20 per hour
- Required project hours: 40

The calculation is:

- Direct labor cost: 40 × $50 = $2,000
- Allocated overhead: 40 × $20 = $800
- Total allocated project cost: $2,800

If the company wants a 20% profit margin on this simplified cost base:

**Required selling price = Total cost ÷ (1 − Target margin)**

$2,800 ÷ 0.80 = $3,500

The corresponding selling price is:

$3,500 ÷ 40 hours = $87.50 per hour

At that price:

- Project revenue: $3,500
- Allocated cost: $2,800
- Project profit before other unallocated items: $700
- Profit margin on price: 20%

The allocation rate helps reveal expenses that might otherwise be overlooked when pricing projects.

**An important caveat:** internal project profitability depends on the chosen allocation method. An allocated cost is not necessarily an incremental cash cost, and a different allocation basis may change the apparent profitability of a project.

## How Overhead Affects Profitability, Cash Flow, and Runway

Overhead affects profitability through expense recognition, but it affects cash flow when payments actually leave the business.

Those events don't always happen at the same time.

A business can recognize revenue today and collect the corresponding cash weeks later.

Likewise, it may recognize an insurance expense over several months while paying the premium in a single annual installment.

This creates a timing difference between accounting performance and cash availability.

### Why a Profitable Business Can Experience a Cash Shortfall

Imagine an agency completes $80,000 of client work during December.

Its simplified accrual-basis income statement shows:

  caption="The agency's simplified accrual-basis income statement for December."
  columns={['Item', 'Amount']}
  rows={[
    ['Revenue earned', '$80,000'],

  numeric={[1]}
/>

The company generated $20,000 in operating profit.

But suppose the $80,000 customer invoice won't be paid until January.

Meanwhile, the business must pay its employees, rent, and other expenses in December.

Its bank balance can decline even while its income statement reports a profit.

Profit answers whether the business earned more than it expensed. Cash flow answers whether it has the money available when payments are due.

### Why Overhead Creates Cash Flow Risk

Some overhead expenses are difficult to reduce quickly.

A company may be contractually committed to:

- Office rent
- Software subscriptions
- Administrative payroll
- Equipment leases
- Insurance payments

If customer collections slow down, those commitments remain.

This is why fixed overhead deserves particular attention in cash flow planning.

### What Does Overhead Mean for Cash Runway?

Cash runway measures how long a company can continue operating before its available cash is exhausted under specified cash flow assumptions.

A simplified runway formula for a business experiencing consistent positive monthly net cash burn is:

**Cash runway = Available cash ÷ Monthly net cash burn**

Suppose:

- Available cash: $120,000
- Monthly cash outflows: $45,000
- Monthly cash inflows: $25,000

Monthly net cash burn = $45,000 − $25,000 = $20,000

Estimated runway = $120,000 ÷ $20,000 = 6 months

This is a simplified estimate. It assumes the net burn rate remains constant and excludes additional timing complications.

If inflows consistently exceed outflows, there is no finite cash exhaustion date under those unchanged assumptions.

Even with a healthy monthly average, however, a business may experience a temporary cash shortage before customer payments arrive.

That is why weekly forecasting matters.

## How to Forecast Overhead Using a 13-Week Cash Flow Forecast

A 13-week cash flow forecast projects expected cash receipts and payments across the next thirteen weeks.

Instead of simply estimating monthly expenses, it places payments in the weeks when they are expected to occur.

This makes it easier to identify periods when overhead payments and customer collections create temporary cash pressure.

### Step 1: Identify Recurring Payments

Start with obligations such as:

- Administrative payroll
- Rent
- Software subscriptions
- Insurance
- Utilities
- General office expenses

Include direct operating payments separately so the forecast captures the business's full anticipated cash requirements.

### Step 2: Assign Payment Dates

A business may have $20,000 in monthly overhead, but that doesn't mean it pays $5,000 every week.

Rent may be due on the first day of the month.

Payroll may clear on the fifteenth.

Software subscriptions may charge on different dates.

The dates matter because cash must be available when each payment settles.

### Step 3: Estimate Customer Collections

<a href="/blog/cash-conversion-cycle" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Forecast when customers are likely to pay</a>, rather than assuming every invoice is collected on its contractual due date.

Use actual payment history, invoice status, contractual terms, and known customer behavior where available.

### Step 4: Calculate Weekly Closing Cash

The basic formula is:

**Closing cash = Opening cash + Cash inflows − Cash outflows**

The closing balance for one week becomes the opening balance for the next.

### Step 5: Compare Expected and Delayed-Payment Scenarios

Consider the following illustrative 13-week forecast for a <a href="/blog/cash-flow-forecasting-for-agencies" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">service agency</a>.

**Starting assumptions**

- Opening cash: $45,000
- Weekly direct service cash costs: $4,500
- Weekly office supplies: $250
- Rent and general marketing: $7,000 combined in Weeks 1, 5, 9, and 13
- Administrative payroll and software: $4,500 combined in Weeks 2, 6, and 10
- Utilities: $500 in Weeks 4, 8, and 12
- Customer collections: $18,000 in Weeks 2, 6, and 10, plus $12,000 in Weeks 4, 8, and 12

For this example, each cost is assumed to settle in the specified week. The weekly total outflows include both direct costs and overhead.

**Scenario A:** All collections arrive as expected.

**Scenario B:** The $18,000 payment expected in Week 2 arrives in Week 6 instead. All other assumptions remain unchanged.

## How to Reduce Overhead Costs Without Hurting Growth

Reducing overhead can improve profitability and cash flow, but cutting every indirect expense is rarely a sustainable strategy.

Some overhead supports productivity, compliance, customer experience, and growth.

The goal should be to eliminate unnecessary spending while preserving the resources that make the business effective.

### 1. Audit Recurring Software Subscriptions

Review recurring software charges and identify:

- Unused subscriptions
- Duplicate tools
- Unnecessary premium plans
- Licenses assigned to inactive users
- Contracts renewing before they are needed

For example, canceling two unused subscriptions costing $150 a month each reduces annual cash spending by $3,600, assuming there are no cancellation fees or replacement costs.

### 2. Review Vendor and Facility Contracts

Examine significant recurring commitments such as office leases, insurance, professional services, and equipment rentals.

Consider whether contract terms still reflect the company's needs.

A lower price is useful, but payment frequency can matter just as much.

For example, an annual software contract may cost less overall but require a large upfront payment. That payment could create short-term liquidity pressure.

### 3. Evaluate Administrative Processes

Repetitive administrative work may be improved through better processes or automation.

Examples include:

- Invoice reconciliation
- Expense categorization
- Internal reporting
- Approval workflows
- Financial data consolidation

Evaluate the full cost of any proposed automation, including implementation, maintenance, training, and integration requirements.

### 4. Forecast Hiring Decisions

Before hiring an administrative employee, <a href="/blog/payroll-forecasting" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">estimate the complete financial commitment</a>.

Include salary, payroll taxes, benefits, equipment, software, and onboarding costs.

Then model the new expenses against expected customer collections.

A hire may be economically justified over the next year but still create a cash shortage during the first several months.

### 5. Review Overhead Alongside Cash Flow

An expense can be affordable on an annual budget yet difficult to pay on its due date.

For every meaningful new commitment, ask:

**What will this expense do to our lowest projected cash balance over the next thirteen weeks?**

That question connects cost management to actual liquidity.

## The Bottom Line

Overhead is more than a collection of rent payments, salaries, and software subscriptions.

It's part of the financial structure that determines how much a business must spend to operate.

Understanding overhead helps owners calculate costs, assess profitability, price projects, and evaluate operating efficiency.

But calculating overhead is only the beginning.

A company can understand its costs perfectly and still experience a cash shortage when customer payments arrive later than expected.

That's why forward-looking cash flow planning matters.

Know what your overhead costs. Then know when it will affect your cash balance.
