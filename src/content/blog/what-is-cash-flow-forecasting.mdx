It shows whether you'll have enough cash to cover payroll, vendors, taxes and loan payments when they're due. Every forecast comes down to four numbers: opening cash, cash in, cash out and closing cash.</p>

## What Is Cash Flow Forecasting?

<p>Here's something I've learned the hard way: a profitable business can still run out of cash.</p>

<p>Say a company invoices $100,000 in October and its expenses total $70,000. On paper that's $30,000 in profit. But customers pay in November, while payroll and suppliers want paying in October. The result: a profitable company that can't pay its bills.</p>

<p>Unlike your <a href="https://www.sec.gov/about/reports-publications/investorpubsbegfinstmtguide" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">P&L statement</a>, which tells you about profitability, a cash flow forecast answers the question that matters for day-to-day survival: <em>"Will we have enough cash when we need to pay the rent, the vendors, our team?"</em></p>

### What is a cash flow forecast?

<p>A cash flow forecast is the document or spreadsheet that comes out of the process: a period-by-period table of your expected cash receipts, cash payments and resulting bank balance. "Cash flow forecasting" is the activity; the "cash flow forecast" is the output.</p>

### What is cash forecasting?

<p>Cash forecasting is another name for cash flow forecasting. Treasury and finance teams often use the shorter term, especially for near-term forecasts covering the next 30, 60 or 90 days. The method is the same: date every expected inflow and outflow and project the balance.</p>

### What does a cash flow forecast predict?

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">It predicts three things you can act on:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Your closing cash balance</strong> for each day, week or month</li>
  <li><strong>Your lowest point</strong>: the week when cash is tightest, and how close it gets to your safety line</li>
  <li><strong>When a shortfall or surplus happens</strong>, so you can move a payment, chase a collection, line up funding or put spare cash to work</li>
</ul>

## Why Cash Flow Forecasting Matters

<p>You could skip forecasting if you're profitable, right? I used to think so too. But profit and cash are different things, and the gap between them is a very common problem. In the Federal Reserve Banks' 2024 Small Business Credit Survey, <strong>51% of US employer firms said uneven cash flows were a financial challenge</strong>, and 56% cited paying operating expenses (<a href="https://doi.org/10.55350/sbcs-20250327" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Federal Reserve Banks, <em>2025 Report on Employer Firms</em></a>).</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">A forecast helps you:</p>

<ul>
  <li>See cash shortages coming before they become crises, instead of scrambling at month-end</li>
  <li>Know if you can actually afford that new hire or equipment purchase</li>
  <li>Understand when outstanding invoices will actually turn into cash</li>
  <li>Plan for funding needs before the situation becomes urgent (lenders often ask to see cash flow projections)</li>
  <li>Test "what if" scenarios: What if collections are 3 weeks late? What if we take on more debt?</li>
</ul>

<p>For startups especially, cash forecasting is lifeline money. It connects every spending decision to your <a href="/blog/runway-vs-burn-rate-for-founders" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">runway</a>, helping you make smarter calls about when to hire, when to slow down and when to raise.</p>

## The Four Building Blocks

<p>Every cash flow forecast is built on four simple pieces. You don't need a fancy financial model to have these.</p>

### 1. Where You Start (Opening Cash)

<p>This is the cash you have at the beginning of your forecast period. If you have $50,000 across all your bank accounts, that's your starting point.</p>

<p><strong>Important:</strong> make sure this is actual available cash, not money tied up in accounts you can't access. I once worked with a company that showed $200,000 on its balance sheet, but $150,000 was in an account it couldn't touch. Its "forecast" was misleading from the start.</p>

### 2. Money Coming In (Cash Inflows)

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">This is cash you expect to receive, not necessarily revenue you've earned. Common examples:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Customer payments (on the date they actually pay, not the invoice date)</li>
  <li>Cash and card sales</li>
  <li>Loan advances, line-of-credit draws or investment capital</li>
  <li>Tax refunds</li>
  <li>Other income you're actually expecting</li>
</ul>

<p>Here's a mistake I see constantly: treating an invoice as cash. Issuing a $20,000 invoice does not increase your bank balance by $20,000. The cash only comes in when your customer actually pays.</p>

### 3. Money Going Out (Cash Outflows)

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">This is cash you expect to pay. For most US businesses that means:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Payroll</strong>, on your actual pay dates (see the <a href="/tools/payroll-calendar" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">payroll calendar</a> for months with three paydays)</li>
  <li><strong>Payroll tax deposits</strong>: depending on your deposit schedule, the IRS requires these monthly or semiweekly</li>
  <li><strong>Estimated tax payments</strong>: for individuals and most pass-through owners, due April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15 (<a href="https://www.irs.gov/faqs/estimated-tax" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">IRS</a>). Corporations pay on the 15th of the 4th, 6th, 9th and 12th months of their tax year</li>
  <li><strong>Sales tax remittances</strong>, if you collect sales tax</li>
  <li>Benefits, health insurance and 401(k) contributions</li>
  <li>Rent, utilities, software subscriptions and insurance</li>
  <li>Vendor and supplier invoices</li>
  <li>Loan and credit card payments</li>
  <li>Equipment and other capital purchases</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Crucial distinction:</strong> timing matters. Your forecast should track when money leaves your bank account, not when you book it as an expense.</p>

### 4. Where You End Up (Closing Cash)

<p>This is what you have left after all inflows and outflows. It also becomes your starting point for the next period.</p>

<p>That's it. Four pieces of information and you have a working forecast.</p>

## The Cash Flow Forecast Formula

<p>Here's how the math works:</p>

<p><strong>Ending Cash = Opening Cash + Cash Inflows − Cash Outflows</strong></p>

<p>A quick example:</p>

<p>A negative net cash flow doesn't mean failure. It just means more cash is leaving than coming in this period. What really matters is whether you have enough cash on hand to cover it.</p>

### The Lesson: Timing Is Everything

<p>This happens all the time. You can have strong sales, great margins and outstanding invoices waiting to be collected, and still miss payroll in a particular week because all your money goes out at once.</p>

<p>For some businesses, even weekly forecasting isn't enough. You might need daily tracking to catch cash gaps within a week.</p>

### What Do You Actually Do With This?

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Once the Week 2 shortfall is visible, you have time to act. Practical options:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Follow up on incoming payments.</strong> Ask customers due around the shortfall whether they can pay earlier.</li>
  <li><strong>Review upcoming payments.</strong> Ask suppliers whether payment dates or terms can move.</li>
  <li><strong>Postpone non-essential spending.</strong> Here, delaying an equipment purchase until after the $28,000 customer payment in Week 3 would ease the pressure.</li>
  <li><strong>Line up short-term funding.</strong> If that isn't enough, evaluate a credit line before the shortfall becomes an emergency.</li>
</ol>

<p>Then update the forecast to see the effect, and keep comparing it against actuals. A forecast doesn't just show you when money might run low. It gives you time to decide.</p>

### Daily and real-time cash forecasting

<p>A daily forecast makes sense when cash is very tight, when you have large payments landing on specific days, or when you manage several bank accounts that need funding before payments go out.</p>

### Weekly forecasts and the 13-week cash flow forecast

<p>A <a href="/blog/what-is-a-13-week-cash-flow-forecast" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">13-week cash flow forecast</a> covers one quarter, week by week. It's detailed enough to catch payroll weeks and late payments, and long enough to see a problem coming with time to fix it. It's also the format lenders most often ask for: many US credit agreements define the required "cash flow forecast" as a 13-week receipts-and-disbursements forecast.</p>

<p>Weekly forecasting suits you if you have weekly or biweekly payroll, large variable expenses, or customers whose payment timing varies.</p>

### Monthly and long-term cash flow forecasting

<p>Monthly forecasts are simpler and better for seeing trends over 3, 6 or 12 months. Long-term forecasts (a year or more) support strategic decisions such as raising capital, taking on debt or opening a new location.</p>

<p><strong>The common sweet spot:</strong> weekly forecasts for the next 12–16 weeks, with a monthly forecast for the next 6–12 months. I've worked with companies that run both and cross-check them. They get operational visibility and strategic planning in one place.</p>

## Seven Steps to Building Your First Forecast

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Here's the good news: you don't need a financial analyst or expensive software to start. You need:</p>

<ul>
  <li>A spreadsheet (Google Sheets or Excel), or our <a href="/tools/13-week-cash-flow-template" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">free 13-week template</a></li>
  <li>Your current bank balances</li>
  <li>An educated guess about the payments and receipts to expect in the coming weeks</li>
</ul>

### Step 1: Pick Your Time Frame

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Decide how far out you want to look and how often you want to see your numbers:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Daily:</strong> best for businesses with very tight cash timing</li>
  <li><strong>Weekly:</strong> great for 3–6 months of operational planning</li>
  <li><strong>Monthly:</strong> good for longer-term budgeting</li>
</ul>

<p>A common approach: weekly forecasts for the next 12–13 weeks, plus a monthly view for the next 6–12 months.</p>

### Step 2: Figure Out Your Starting Cash

<p>Grab your latest bank statements and add up what you can actually spend. If you have multiple accounts, include them all, and watch for double-counting transfers. This is your "day 1" number.</p>

### Step 3: List the Money Coming In

<p>Look at your accounts receivable, expected customer payments, recurring subscriptions, loan draws and other income.</p>

<p>Pro tip: think about how your customers <em>actually</em> pay, not just when they <em>should</em> pay. If they typically pay 15 days late, build that in instead of using invoice due dates.</p>

### Step 4: List the Money Going Out

<p>Payroll, payroll tax deposits, rent, vendor invoices, estimated and sales taxes, loan payments, utilities, software subscriptions and equipment purchases. Put each one on the date it will actually leave your account.</p>

<p>Remember: not every expense is a cash payment. Depreciation is a non-cash expense, so don't count it here.</p>

### Step 5: Crunch the Numbers

<p>For each period, calculate:</p>

<p><strong>Ending Cash = Opening Cash + Receipts − Payments</strong></p>

<p>The ending balance for Week 1 becomes the opening balance for Week 2, and so on. Keep rolling forward.</p>

### Step 6: Run Different Scenarios

<p>Here's where most people give up: "Too complicated," they say. But a single forecast can hide what's coming. A customer might pay late, costs might rise, revenue might fall short.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Build at least three versions:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Base case:</strong> what you expect to happen</li>
  <li><strong>Worst case:</strong> some payments are late, some expenses are higher</li>
  <li><strong>Best case:</strong> payments come in early, revenue beats expectations</li>
</ul>

<p>Track not just the ending balance but the <strong>lowest point</strong> in each scenario.</p>

### Step 7: Watch It Update

<p>Here's the key to making this useful: compare it to reality. At the end of each week, replace what you forecast with what actually happened.</p>

<p>Why was an invoice collected late? Did a vendor overcharge you? Was the timing different than planned? Your next forecast is smarter because you learned from last week's data.</p>

## Cash Flow Forecasting Methods

<p>There are two primary ways to build a cash flow forecast, plus a few techniques for keeping it current and testing it.</p>

### Direct method (receipts and disbursements)

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">This is what most people should start with. You list individual cash receipts and payments by date:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Invoice collections</li>
  <li>Payroll and payroll taxes</li>
  <li>Rent</li>
  <li>Vendor bills</li>
  <li>Loan payments</li>
  <li>Taxes</li>
</ul>

<p>It's especially powerful for short-term (weekly) forecasting because it focuses on when cash actually moves.</p>

### Indirect method: adjusted net income and pro forma balance sheet

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">The indirect method starts with projected profit and adjusts for non-cash items and balance sheet changes:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Depreciation and amortization (non-cash expenses)</li>
  <li>Changes in accounts receivable</li>
  <li>Changes in accounts payable and inventory</li>
  <li>Other working capital adjustments</li>
</ul>

<p>There are two common variations. The <strong>adjusted net income</strong> approach starts from projected net income and works down to cash. The <strong>pro forma balance sheet</strong> approach projects the whole balance sheet and reads the cash line from it. Finance teams sometimes also use an <em>accrual reversal</em> method for medium-term forecasts.</p>

<p>Because it ties to your P&L and balance sheet (and to the cash flow statement format US companies report under ASC 230), the indirect method is more useful for longer-term planning.</p>

<p><strong>My advice:</strong> start with direct and add indirect later. The direct method is easier to understand and more accurate for short-term cash management.</p>

### Rolling forecasts

<p>A rolling forecast "rolls forward" as time passes. In a 13-week rolling forecast, each week you replace that week's forecast with actual results, update your assumptions and add a new week at the end. You're always looking at the next 13 weeks instead of re-forecasting to the end of a quarter.</p>

### Scenario and sensitivity analysis

<p>Scenarios (base, worst and best case) change several assumptions at once. Sensitivity analysis changes <strong>one</strong> assumption to see how much it matters. For example: "If our two biggest customers each pay two weeks late, what happens to our lowest week?" Run it on the assumptions that scare you most.</p>

### Predictive (statistical and AI) cash flow forecasting

<p>Predictive forecasting uses your historical data, such as each customer's actual payment history, to estimate <em>when</em> receipts will arrive instead of assuming the due date. It typically needs a reasonable history of invoices and bank transactions to work well. It's most useful when you have many customers with different payment habits.</p>

<p>Read our full guide to <a href="/blog/predictive-cash-flow-forecasting" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">predictive cash flow forecasting</a> to see how dating receipts by real payment behavior improves your view of the weeks ahead.</p>

### A practical approach

<p>Use a <strong>13-week direct-method rolling forecast</strong> as your primary tool. Most businesses need to know the next 3–4 months. The direct method makes it clear when cash moves, and the rolling approach keeps it current.</p>

## How to Forecast Cash Flow When a Few Large Invoices Land Late

<p>This is one of the most common questions I hear: <em>"Our cash swings month to month because a few large customer invoices land late. How do we forecast around that?"</em></p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">When a handful of customers make up a big share of your receipts, an average collection assumption will mislead you. Here's what to do:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>List your largest customers separately.</strong> Don't bury them in an "average collections" line. Give each big invoice its own row.</li>
  <li><strong>Date each one from that customer's actual history.</strong> If a customer's last six payments arrived 12–25 days after the due date, forecast them 20 days late, not on the due date.</li>
  <li><strong>Run an on-time case and a late case</strong> for each big invoice, and track the lowest balance in the late case.</li>
  <li><strong>Set a trigger balance.</strong> Decide in advance what you'll do if the late case drops below your safety line: draw on the credit line, delay a purchase, or call the customer.</li>
  <li><strong>Reduce the swing at the source.</strong> Ask for deposits or milestone billing on large projects, offer card or ACH payment to speed collection, and stagger supplier payments around your biggest receipts.</li>
</ol>

## How to Measure Cash Forecast Accuracy

<p>No forecast is ever 100% accurate. The goal is to know <em>how</em> wrong it is, and in which direction, so it gets better every week.</p>

### The forecast variance formula

<p>For each line (or each week's total), calculate:</p>

<p><strong>Forecast variance % = (Actual − Forecast) ÷ Forecast × 100</strong></p>

<p>Here's a four-week check on cash inflows:</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="Inflows came in 12.3% below forecast over four weeks: an optimistic bias."
  columns={['Week', 'Forecast inflows', 'Actual inflows', 'Variance']}
  numeric={[1, 2, 3]}
  rows={[
    ['1', '$5,000', '$4,200', '−16.0%'],
    ['2', '$2,000', '$2,000', '0.0%'],
    ['3', '$28,000', '$22,000', '−21.4%'],
    ['4', '$8,000', '$9,500', '+18.8%'],
    ['Total', '$43,000', '$37,700', '−12.3%'],
  ]}
/>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Two numbers matter:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Error size:</strong> how far off you were, ignoring direction. Here the average weekly miss is about 14%.</li>
  <li><strong>Bias:</strong> whether you're consistently too optimistic or too cautious. Here total inflows came in 12.3% below forecast, which means the forecast was optimistic. That's the dangerous direction.</li>
</ul>

### How to improve your accuracy

<ol>
  <li><strong>Start with clean data.</strong> Keep bank statements up to date, reconcile accounts monthly, and keep receivables and payables current. Garbage in means garbage out.</li>
  <li><strong>Model how people actually pay.</strong> Don't just use contract terms. In my experience, historical collection data is more accurate than contract terms. People's behavior is consistent even when it contradicts what they say they'll do.</li>
  <li><strong>Track accuracy separately</strong> for inflows and outflows, and at different horizons (next week vs. four weeks out vs. thirteen weeks out). Near-term weeks should be tighter.</li>
  <li><strong>Investigate every big miss.</strong> Expected $25,000 from Customer A and collected $18,000? Find out why. Expected a vendor payment on the 15th and it went out on the 5th? Fix the date in next week's forecast.</li>
  <li><strong>Keep iterating.</strong> If your typical customer pays in 21 days instead of 30, use that.</li>
</ol>

<p>It's not about a more complex spreadsheet. It's about better inputs.</p>

## Cash Flow Forecast vs. Cash Flow Statement vs. Budget vs. Cash Management

<p>These are often confused, but they do different jobs.</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="A statement looks back, a forecast looks ahead, a budget sets targets, and cash management acts on the forecast."
  columns={['Tool', 'What it does', 'Time frame', 'Typically owned by']}
  rows={[
    ['Cash flow forecast', 'Predicts future cash in, cash out and balance', 'Next 13 weeks to 12+ months', 'Founder, finance lead, CFO'],
    ['Cash flow statement', 'Reports cash that actually moved (a financial statement under ASC 230)', 'Last month, quarter or year', 'Accountant or controller'],
    ['Budget', 'Sets revenue and spending targets', 'Usually the year ahead', 'Leadership team'],
    ['Cash management', "Optimizes today's cash: payments, collections, idle balances, account sweeps", 'Today to the next few weeks', 'Finance or treasury'],
    ['Liquidity planning', 'Makes sure obligations can always be met, including credit lines and reserves', 'Months to years', 'CFO or treasury'],
  ]}
/>

<p>In short: a statement looks back, a forecast looks ahead, a budget sets targets, and cash management and liquidity planning act on what the forecast shows.</p>

<p>You can stay within your monthly expense budget and still run out of cash because your receivables are tied up. That's why none of these replaces a forward-looking cash forecast.</p>

## Common Cash Flow Forecasting Mistakes

<p>I've seen all of these, sometimes in the same company.</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Treating invoices as cash.</strong> If you assume an invoice is paid on day 30 and it arrives on day 45, the forecast looks healthy until you're short on day 31.</li>
  <li><strong>Spreading expenses evenly.</strong> Don't divide monthly costs by four. Rent is due on the 1st, payroll is biweekly, vendors are net-30, and estimated taxes land on fixed dates. Forecast the cash timing, not the accounting expense.</li>
  <li><strong>Only looking at month-end balances.</strong> Always track your lowest projected balance during the month.</li>
  <li><strong>No safety cushion.</strong> If you need $10,000 to operate and the forecast shows $5,000, you're already in a hole. Build in a realistic reserve.</li>
  <li><strong>Building a forecast and then ignoring it.</strong> A spreadsheet untouched for months is useless. Make it a weekly habit.</li>
</ol>

## Spreadsheet, QuickBooks or Cash Forecasting Software?

<p>You don't need a CFO or expensive software to start. A well-maintained spreadsheet works well for early-stage startups, businesses with simple cash timing, and teams still building the habit. It's flexible, transparent and free.</p>

### Can you forecast cash flow in QuickBooks?

<p>Partly. QuickBooks Online includes a <strong>Cash Flow Planner</strong> that projects your cash over roughly the next 90 days from your connected bank accounts and QuickBooks data, and lets you add planned items (<a href="https://quickbooks.intuit.com/learn-support/en-us/help-article/budget-forecast-reports/use-cash-flow-planner-quickbooks-online/L2l59mIqe_US_en_US" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Intuit</a>). QuickBooks Desktop's older Cash Flow Projector looked about six weeks ahead and was discontinued in QuickBooks Desktop 2022 (<a href="https://quickbooks.intuit.com/learn-support/en-us/help-article/accounts-payable/set-cash-flow-projector-quickbooks-desktop/L0BSJYlHq_US_en_US" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Intuit</a>).</p>

<p>It's a useful starting point, but most teams outgrow it when they need a full 13-week weekly view, scenario comparisons or alerts. We cover the details in <a href="/blog/can-quickbooks-forecast-cash-flow" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Can QuickBooks forecast cash flow?</a></p>

### What a cash forecasting model should include (checklist)

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Whatever tool you use, a good model has:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Opening balance from reconciled bank accounts (accessible cash only)</li>
  <li>Receivables dated by actual customer payment behavior</li>
  <li>Payables and recurring bills on their real due dates</li>
  <li>Payroll on actual pay dates, plus payroll tax deposits</li>
  <li>Estimated tax and sales tax payments</li>
  <li>Loan payments, credit line draws and repayments</li>
  <li>Planned capital purchases</li>
  <li>A minimum cash (safety line) threshold</li>
  <li>Base, worst and best-case scenarios</li>
  <li>Forecast vs. actual variance, updated weekly</li>
</ul>

### When software makes sense

<p>Consider software when you spend more time maintaining the forecast than learning from it: copying data by hand, falling behind on weekly updates, struggling to compare scenarios. Good cash flow software pulls from your bank and accounting system, updates as transactions post, compares scenarios and alerts you when cash drops below your threshold.</p>

<p><strong>My advice:</strong> start with a spreadsheet and build the process first. The goal isn't buying software. It's freeing yourself to focus on the decisions.</p>

## Who Uses Cash Flow Forecasting?

<ul>
  <li><strong>Small businesses and startups</strong> use it to make payroll, manage runway and decide when to hire. See <a href="/blog/will-i-make-payroll" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Will I make payroll?</a></li>
  <li><strong>Agencies and project-based firms</strong> use it to manage the gap between client payments and payroll, especially with retainers and milestone billing.</li>
  <li><strong>SaaS and subscription businesses</strong> use it to place annual and quarterly contract payments on the week they actually hit the bank, rather than spreading them evenly by month.</li>
  <li><strong>Corporate finance and treasury teams</strong> use it to manage daily cash positions across multiple entities and accounts, funding needs and debt covenants.</li>
  <li><strong>Lenders</strong> use it to assess repayment ability. Many credit agreements require borrowers to deliver a 13-week cash flow forecast and update it regularly.</li>
</ul>

## The Bottom Line

<p>The Week 2 dip wasn't hypothetical for us. At our startup, the weekly forecast showed exactly that drop while month-end looked fine. Because we saw it early, we renegotiated supplier terms, moved $10,000 from reserves to cover payroll, delayed an equipment purchase by three months, and set up a short-term credit line before we needed it.</p>

<p>That's the point of cash flow forecasting. It isn't about predicting the future perfectly. It's about avoiding a cash surprise at payroll time. Keep your forecast timely (update it weekly), detailed (weekly balances, not just month-end) and responsive (adjust to what actually happens). The most important number isn't your month-end balance. It's the <strong>lowest cash balance you'll hit in any week</strong>.</p>
