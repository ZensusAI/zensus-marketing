## The Problem with How Businesses Think About Cash

<p>Most businesses have a fundamental misunderstanding: they confuse expected revenue with available cash.</p>

<p>A signed contract won't pay your salaries. An invoice won't cover your rent. And even a profitable month can contain a brutal week where you're scrambling to keep the lights on.</p>

<p>Financial statements can show a company is doing great while its actual bank balance tells a completely different story.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Why? Because revenue, profit, and cash measure three different things:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Revenue</strong> is what you've earned.</li>
  <li><strong>Profit</strong> is what's left after expenses (according to accounting rules).</li>
  <li><strong>Cash</strong> is what's actually in the bank to make payments.</li>
</ul>

<p>This difference matters especially when customers pay on 30, 45, or 60 day terms. The <a href="/blog/cash-conversion-cycle" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">cash conversion cycle</a> measures how long that gap ties up your cash.</p>

<p><strong>Predictive cash flow forecasting</strong> attempts to close that gap. Instead of just recording what already happened, it projects what could happen to your cash balance over the coming weeks and months.</p>

<p>It combines expected customer payments, recurring expenses, payroll, what's already in the bank, and some assumptions about the future to estimate how much cash you'll have on any given date. If you are new to the idea, <a href="/blog/what-is-cash-flow-forecasting" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">what is cash flow forecasting?</a> covers the basics and the methods.</p>

<p>A useful forecast doesn't just answer "Is the business making money?" It answers the immediate, scary question: <strong>"Will we have enough cash when we need it?"</strong></p>

## The Cash Timing Trap: An Illustrative Scenario

<p>Imagine you're running a business with $250,000 in the bank, $80,000 in monthly operating expenses, and $110,000 in outstanding invoices.</p>

<p>On paper, everything looks solid. Customers are paying on time, revenue is growing, and you have plenty of cash to keep the lights on.</p>

<p><strong>Then a $65,000 payment from a key customer, originally due on November 9, gets delayed until November 30.</strong></p>

<p>Payroll hits your books on November 13. Software subscriptions are automatically charged the following week. You've also promised a contractor some money before the month's out.</p>

<p>The business itself hasn't changed. The same revenue's coming in. The same contracts are still valid.</p>

<p>But the timing is off. And that three-week delay could completely throw off your cash flow.</p>

<p>Depending on when all the other payments land, that $65,000 pushback might mean you don't have enough liquidity to cover what you owe in these three weeks.</p>

<p>This sounds like a hypothetical, but it happens more often than you'd think.</p>

<p><strong>The real question isn't whether you'll ever get that money. It's whether you can actually make payroll, pay the bills, and keep going until it arrives.</strong> The guide to <a href="/blog/will-i-make-payroll" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">knowing whether you will make payroll</a> turns that question into a checklist.</p>

<p>You measure a forecast by comparing what it predicted against what actually happened.</p>

<p>The forecast was off by $15,000, or 12.5% of the forecast amount.</p>

<p>But that number alone doesn't tell you why. Maybe a customer paid two weeks late. Maybe a vendor invoice was bigger than expected. Maybe you hired someone earlier than planned.</p>

<p><strong>Those distinctions matter.</strong></p>

<p>A forecast that's wrong because receivables ran late requires a different fix than a forecast that underestimated expenses.</p>

<p>One practical approach? Maintain a <a href="/blog/what-is-a-13-week-cash-flow-forecast" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline"><strong>rolling 13-week cash flow forecast</strong></a>.</p>

<p>At the end of each week, compare your original forecast against what actually happened: cash received, cash paid, and closing balance.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Then dig into the biggest differences. I'd look for three things:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Which payments arrived later than expected?</li>
  <li>Which expenses were higher or came sooner than forecast?</li>
  <li>Are the same errors happening over and over?</li>
</ul>

<p>The goal isn't to hit zero variance; that's unrealistic. The goal is to <strong>learn</strong>: Is your forecast getting better at representing how your business actually operates?</p>

<p>A forecast that consistently overestimates incoming payments is telling you something important about your assumptions, before a cash shortage happens.</p>

## Why Forecast a Range Instead of One Number?

<p>A single forecast line can make an uncertain future look more predictable than it really is.</p>

<p>Imagine a business with $200,000 in cash, receiving $75,000 in customer payments monthly, and spending $90,000 a month.</p>

<p>At that rate, they're losing $15,000 in cash each month. That's the expected case.</p>

<p>But what if collections slow down? What if a major client stops paying? What if expenses jump unexpectedly?</p>

<p>A single forecast line doesn't communicate those risks.</p>

<p>Instead, think in <strong>scenarios</strong>:</p>

<p><strong>None of this predicts the future.</strong> It just shows how different assumptions affect your financial position.</p>

<p>The key: your worst-case scenario should be specific enough to explain what could actually go wrong.</p>

<p>Losing your largest customer is meaningful. Arbitrarily reducing revenue by 30% without a reason isn't helpful.</p>

<p>A forecast becomes more valuable when scenarios reflect real decisions or risks the business recognizes.</p>

<p>If losing a major customer would leave you with only $50,000 in cash after three months, management can examine that risk before it becomes a reality.</p>

<p>That might mean delaying a hire, renegotiating payment terms, or building a larger cash reserve.</p>

<p><strong>The point of forecasting isn't to predict one outcome. It's to prepare for several.</strong></p>

## When Does Money Actually Arrive?

<p>The date money arrives can matter far more than the date revenue is earned.</p>

<p>Consider a small services business that invoices a customer $40,000 on November 2, 2026.</p>

<p>The invoice has 30-day payment terms, making it due on December 2.</p>

<p>But the customer typically pays 15 days after the due date.</p>

<p>That changes the expected cash receipt date to <strong>December 17</strong>.</p>

<p>That's a cash timing problem, even when the underlying work is profitable.</p>

<p>A traditional forecast based on monthly revenue might treat the $40,000 as a November inflow.</p>

<p>A better cash forecast uses the date the payment is realistically expected to arrive.</p>

<p>And when payment behavior changes, the forecast should change with it.</p>

## When Does Money Actually Leave?

<p>Forecasting receivables is only half the battle. A business also needs to know exactly when its obligations will reduce the cash balance.</p>

<p>Expenses don't leave the bank in neat monthly averages.</p>

<p>Payroll might hit twice a month. Rent may be due on the 1st. Contractors might be paid after milestones. Annual subscriptions can create weirdly large one-time withdrawals.</p>

<p>Imagine this week:</p>

<p>A business starts Monday, November 9 with $35,000 in cash.</p>

<p>It expects a $30,000 customer payment on Friday, November 13.</p>

<p>But payroll of $28,000 is scheduled for Wednesday, November 11, and a vendor payment of $12,000 is due Thursday, November 12.</p>

<p>The cash position looks like this:</p>

<p>But on Thursday, its projected balance falls to <strong>negative $5,000</strong>.</p>

<p>That shortfall exists even though the business ends the week with a positive forecast balance.</p>

<p><strong>A company cannot wait until Friday to pay obligations due on Thursday.</strong></p>

<p>And a bank account doesn't automatically permit a negative balance. Without a financing arrangement, the business could face failed payments, penalties, or delayed obligations.</p>

<p>This is why monthly averages can be dangerously misleading.</p>

<p>A forecast showing only the month-end balance might completely miss a cash shortage in the middle of the month.</p>

<p>Even a weekly closing balance can hide a shortage that occurs between payment dates.</p>

<p>For businesses with tight liquidity, the forecast needs enough detail to reveal those <strong>critical days</strong>. <p><strong>The question isn't just how much the company spends. It's when those payments compete for the same dollars.</strong></p>

## How Do Cash Flow Forecasts Actually Fail?

<p>Forecasts usually fail when the assumptions behind them stop reflecting reality.</p>

<p>And the most dangerous errors are often the ones that appear small at first.</p>

### The Customer Who Always Paid Late

<p>A design agency forecasts $80,000 in customer collections for the month.</p>

<p>Historically, its largest customer pays about two weeks after the contractual due date. But the forecast assumes the payment will arrive on time.</p>

<p>The expected $35,000 payment doesn't arrive before payroll.</p>

<p>The agency has to delay a planned $20,000 contractor payment and arrange temporary financing to bridge the gap.</p>

<p>The forecast wasn't wrong about how much the customer owed. It was wrong about <strong>when</strong> the money would arrive.</p>

<p>That difference created an avoidable cash management problem. <a href="/blog/cash-flow-forecasting-for-agencies" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Cash flow forecasting for agencies</a> works through this pattern in more detail.</p>

### The Expense That Wasn't in the Forecast

<p>A growing software company expects to end the quarter with $95,000 in cash.</p>

<p>Its forecast includes regular payroll, cloud infrastructure, and software subscriptions.</p>

<p>But it excludes a <strong>$24,000 annual vendor renewal</strong> scheduled for the final month.</p>

<p>The company finishes the quarter with $71,000 instead of $95,000.</p>

<p>That's a <strong>25.3% shortfall</strong> relative to the original projected balance.</p>

<p>A management team relying on the original forecast might have approved an additional expense or hiring commitment that its actual cash position couldn't comfortably support.</p>

<p>The problem wasn't an unpredictable market event. It was a known obligation missing from the model.</p>

<p><strong>Both examples lead to the same lesson:</strong> A cash flow forecast is only as reliable as the assumptions and payment schedules behind it.</p>

<p>A forecast should be treated as a <strong>working model</strong>, not a static financial document.</p>

<p>When customers change their payment behavior, expenses increase, or the business makes new commitments, those changes need to appear in the numbers.</p>

## How Do You Start Forecasting Your Cash Flow This Week?

<p>You don't need a complex financial model to begin.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Start with a spreadsheet and one hour of focused work:</p>

<ol>
  <li>Write down the amount of cash available in your business accounts today.</li>
  <li>List the payments you reasonably expect to receive over the next 13 weeks. Include the customer, expected amount, and likely payment date.</li>
  <li>Record every known cash obligation during the same period. Include payroll, rent, contractors, debt payments, subscriptions, taxes, and any larger one-time expenses.</li>
  <li>Group these movements by week.</li>
  <li>For each week, calculate: <strong>Ending cash = Beginning cash + Cash received − Cash paid</strong></li>
  <li>Carry the ending balance forward as the next week's beginning balance.</li>
  <li>Once you've completed the 13 weeks, find the week with the <strong>lowest projected cash balance</strong>. That's the first number I would examine.</li>
  <li>Then ask: <strong>What happens if your largest expected customer payment arrives two weeks late?</strong></li>
  <li>Move that receipt to the later week and recalculate the balances.</li>
  <li>You now have two forecasts: one based on current expectations and another showing the impact of a specific payment delay.</li>
</ol>

<p>The free <a href="/tools/13-week-cash-flow-template" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">13-week cash flow template</a> has these columns ready, and <a href="/blog/how-to-build-a-13-week-cash-flow-forecast-in-excel" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">how to build a 13-week cash flow forecast in Excel</a> walks through the formulas.</p>

<p>Neither forecast will be perfect.</p>

<p>But both are more useful than assuming every invoice will be paid exactly when you need the money.</p>

<p>From there, make the forecast a <strong>weekly habit</strong>.</p>

<p>Replace projected numbers with actual payments, record the differences, and update the assumptions that proved inaccurate.</p>

<p>Over time, the model should become a more accurate reflection of how your business collects and spends money.</p>

<p>The first forecast establishes your assumptions. Every update tests them.</p>
