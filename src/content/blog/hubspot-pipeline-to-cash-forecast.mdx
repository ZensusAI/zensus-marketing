<p id="blog-article-lead" className="text-lg leading-relaxed text-muted-foreground">So you just checked your HubSpot dashboard. It says you've billed $60,000 this month. Pretty solid, right?</p>

<p>Then you glance at your bank account... and that's when the reality check hits.</p>

<p>Some customers haven't paid yet. Others are still within their payment terms. A subscription renewal is scheduled for next week, but you're nervous: will it clear on time? And there's this payment marked as "successful" that... actually, has it really made it to the bank?</p>

<p>Here's the truth: <strong>you can have the revenue in HubSpot, but not the cash in the bank.</strong></p>

<p>That's the problem. And if you're trying to <a href="/blog/what-is-cash-flow-forecasting" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">forecast cash flow</a> from HubSpot alone, you're going to end up more confused than when you started.</p>

## The Simple Truth: HubSpot Shows Revenue, Not Cash

<p>To forecast actual cash flow from HubSpot, you need to start with something more concrete than sales reports: <strong>open invoices, outstanding balances, due dates, and scheduled subscription billing.</strong></p>

<p>Then you estimate when each payment will <em>actually</em> arrive: not when it's due, not when the contract is signed, but when that money hits your bank. Adjust for how your customers typically pay, factor in processing delays, and make sure the receipts line up with what your bank shows.</p>

<p>The result? A cash collection forecast that tells you not just "how much" customers owe, but <em>when</em> that money will be available to spend.</p>

## Here's Where Most People Go Wrong

### Revenue ≠ Cash (And HubSpot Reports Differently)

<p><a href="https://knowledge.hubspot.com/reports/report-on-revenue" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">HubSpot's documentation</a> is pretty clear about this: it separates pipeline forecasts, booked revenue, billed revenue, and collected revenue. Each represents a different stage of the financial lifecycle.</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="From a sales deal to money in the bank: only the last stage is cash you have."
  columns={['Stage', 'What It Means', 'Does It Mean You Have Cash?']}
  rows={[
    ['Sales deal', "Someone's interested", 'Nope'],
    ['Contract signed', "They're committed", 'Nope'],
    ['Invoice sent', 'You sent the bill', 'Nope'],
    ['Payment sent', "They're making payment", 'Maybe'],
    ['Payment succeeded', 'HubSpot sees it', 'Maybe'],
    ['Money in the bank', "It's actually yours", 'Yes'],
  ]}
/>

<p>Let me give you an example that matters.</p>

<p>Imagine you land a <strong>$24,000 annual software contract in October</strong>. The invoice goes out in November. Your customer has Net 30 payment terms, but they typically pay about ten days late.</p>

<p>That means your deal closed in October, but you probably won't see the cash until <strong>December</strong>.</p>

<p>If you're forecasting that entire $24,000 as October cash, you're painting a rosier picture than reality. And that's risky when you're budgeting, covering payroll, or planning investments.</p>

## What Data Do You Actually Need?

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">You don't need every single field in HubSpot to build a useful cash forecast. Focus on the three questions that matter:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>How much money is still expected?</strong></li>
  <li><strong>When will it likely arrive?</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Has it already been collected or deposited?</strong></li>
</ol>

<p>Three types of records will do the heavy lifting.</p>

### 1. Invoices: Your Short-Term Collection Radar

<p>Start with your invoices. An invoice is a concrete billing event: you know exactly what you billed and when.</p>

<p><a href="https://knowledge.hubspot.com/invoices/manage-invoices" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">HubSpot tracks invoice statuses</a> like Draft, Open, Paid, and Void. Here's what they mean for forecasting:</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="How to treat each HubSpot invoice status in a cash forecast."
  columns={['Status', 'Action']}
  rows={[
    [<strong>Draft</strong>, "Skip it for now, unless you're certain it's going to get sent"],
    [<strong>Open</strong>, 'Include it in your forecast (minus any payments already received)'],
    [<strong>Paid</strong>, 'Already collected: match it against your bank records'],
    [<strong>Void</strong>, "Don't count it"],
  ]}
/>

<p>Here's the thing many people miss: <strong>an "Open" invoice doesn't mean nothing's been paid yet.</strong></p>

<p>For example, your customer might have paid $4,000 against a $10,000 invoice. The invoice still shows as "Open," but your forecast shouldn't count the full $10,000. It should only forecast the remaining $6,000. Otherwise, you're counting the same money twice.</p>

### 2. Subscriptions: The "Predictable but Risky" Category

<p>Unlike invoices, subscriptions can help you estimate <em>future</em> recurring billing. But they're not a cash crystal ball.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Imagine two SaaS companies, both reporting <strong>$12,000 in <a href="/blog/arr-vs-cash-for-founders" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR)</a></strong>:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Company A</strong> collects $12,000 every single month</li>
  <li><strong>Company B</strong> collects $144,000 annually (one big upfront payment)</li>
</ul>

<p>Same MRR, but their cash flow patterns are worlds apart. Company A gets smaller, frequent inflows. Company B gets hit with one big payment once a year, then months with little from that contract.</p>

<p>If you just look at MRR, both might appear to collect $12,000 monthly. Wrong for Company B.</p>

<p>To forecast subscription cash correctly, you need to preserve the <strong>actual billing frequency</strong> and account for <strong>real-world collection timing</strong>.</p>

<p><a href="https://knowledge.hubspot.com/subscriptions/manage-subscriptions" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">HubSpot subscription statuses</a> (Active, Scheduled, Unpaid, Paused, Canceled, Expired) each need different treatment:</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="How to treat each HubSpot subscription status in a cash forecast."
  columns={['Status', 'How to Handle']}
  rows={[
    [<strong>Active</strong>, 'Project future billing, but factor in realistic collection timing'],
    [<strong>Scheduled</strong>, 'Start projecting from the actual start date'],
    [<strong>Unpaid</strong>, "Don't count it in your baseline: something's wrong"],
    [<strong>Paused</strong>, 'Exclude billing during pause periods'],
    [<strong>Canceled</strong>, 'Stop projecting after cancellation'],
    [<strong>Expired</strong>, "Only count what's already been billed"],
  ]}
/>

### 3. Payment Records: The "Almost There" Zone

<p>This is where most forecasts get fuzzy.</p>

<p>A payment showing as "Succeeded" in HubSpot <strong>doesn't always mean</strong> the money's in your bank yet.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Processing:</strong> The customer clicked pay, but nothing's confirmed yet</li>
  <li><strong>Succeeded:</strong> Payment processor says "done," but has the money left the payment processor?</li>
  <li><strong>Failed:</strong> Back to square one. Figure out if they'll retry</li>
</ul>

<p>When you <a href="https://knowledge.hubspot.com/payments/manage-payments" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">manually enter payments</a>, a "Succeeded" status might just mean you recorded it, not that the funds have arrived.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">For an accurate forecast, track <strong>two separate dates</strong>:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Payment date:</strong> When the transaction happens</li>
  <li><strong>Expected bank availability:</strong> When you can actually spend that money</li>
</ul>

<p>This difference becomes critical if you have <a href="/blog/will-i-make-payroll" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">payroll due</a> in three days and the payment clears in five days. <strong>Spoiler: you're short cash.</strong></p>

## Here's Why Simple Due Dates Don't Cut It

<p>So many forecasts start with invoice due dates. On paper, that seems reasonable. But it's where the cracks form.</p>

<p>A due date tells you when a customer <em>should</em> pay. It doesn't tell you when they <em>will</em> pay.</p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground"><strong>Example:</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li>Invoice due: November 6</li>
  <li>Customer historically: 10 days late</li>
  <li>Forecast (using due date only): Nov 6 ✗</li>
  <li>Forecast (adjusted for behavior): Nov 16 ✓</li>
</ul>

<p>That ten-day difference could mean the difference between making payroll on time or scrambling to find emergency funding.</p>

<p>Here's what works better:</p>

<p>That feedback becomes gold.</p>

## If You're Using HubSpot + QuickBooks + Stripe... Here's the Trap

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">So you've got:</p>

<ul>
  <li>CRM/sales data in <a href="/integrations/hubspot" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">HubSpot</a></li>
  <li>Invoices in <a href="/integrations/quickbooks" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">QuickBooks</a></li>
  <li>Payments going through Stripe</li>
  <li>Cash in your bank account</li>
</ul>

<p>All these systems have records of the same underlying transactions. If you just dump them all into one forecast, you'll massively overstate your cash.</p>

### The Solution: Link Your Records

<p>Think of it like this: one subscription renewal doesn't equal five separate cash inflows.</p>

<BlogTable
  caption="Linking records so one renewal is counted as one collection."
  columns={['Step', 'What Happens']}
  rows={[
    ['Subscription is active', 'Plan a billing event'],
    ['Invoice is generated', 'Replace the planned event with the actual invoice'],
    ['Payment is made', 'Reduce the outstanding invoice amount'],
    ['Money hits bank', 'Reconcile against your forecast'],
  ]}
/>

<p>Here's where it gets tricky: a QuickBooks payment might apply across multiple invoices, and <a href="https://knowledge.hubspot.com/integrations/connect-hubspot-and-quickbooks-online" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">HubSpot doesn't sync those payments</a>. That's why manual reconciliation matters.</p>

### Payment Success ≠ Bank Cash

<p>Let's say your customer pays $10,000 on Friday. Stripe marks it as successful. But Stripe doesn't transfer funds to your bank until Wednesday of next week.</p>

<p>If you count that money as "available" on Friday and have a $5,000 payroll due Monday... well, you might be looking at a cash shortfall.</p>

<p><strong>The bank balance is what you can actually spend. Your forecast estimates what will come next.</strong></p>

## A Note on HubSpot's Own Limits

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">HubSpot's <a href="https://knowledge.hubspot.com/reports/report-on-revenue" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">revenue reporting</a> is solid for what they track. But for a cash forecast, you need:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Expected <strong>bank receipt dates</strong> (not just payment statuses)</li>
  <li>Your <strong>actual cash balance</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Payment settlement timing</strong> (how long from success to deposit)</li>
  <li>Your <strong>cash outflows</strong> (payroll, vendors, taxes, etc.)</li>
</ul>

<p>These details vary based on your HubSpot configuration, your accounting system, and your payment processor.</p>
