<p id="blog-article-lead" className="text-lg leading-relaxed text-muted-foreground">Let's be honest: <strong>you can be profitable for the whole quarter and still run out of cash next Friday.</strong></p>

<p>A marketing agency might have delivered the work, sent the invoice, recorded the revenue in their books, and even shown a nice profit on the P&L. But that doesn't mean the money's actually in the bank when you need it. Meanwhile, your payroll is due in exactly two weeks, your software vendor is chalking up that monthly charge, and that ad platform requires payment before the client reimburses you.</p>

<p>That's where cash flow forecasting comes in. It's not about predicting <em>how much</em> profit you'll make. It's about answering a terrifyingly simple question: <strong>What's the lowest bank balance we expect over the next 13 weeks? And when do we need to act before that happens?</strong></p>

## What Is Cash Flow Forecasting for Agencies?

<p>Here's the thing: a cash flow forecast for agencies is about <em>timing</em>. It's a date-based plan of when money comes in and goes out.</p>

<p>You start with the cash you have in the bank <em>right now</em>, add up the client payments you expect to see in the weeks ahead, and subtract all the money you're obligated to pay when it's actually due, not when you <em>wish</em> it were due.</p>

<p>The formula? Simple:</p>

<p>Closing cash = Opening cash + cash receipts that clear − cash payments that leave</p>

<p>Repeat that for 13 weeks. Compare each week's ending balance with your <a href="/blog/cash-flow-early-warning-system" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">minimum operating cash floor</a>. If you dip below that floor, you have a problem before you even hit quarter-end.</p>

<p>Here's where agencies get themselves into trouble: a project can have an attractive margin but still burn cash during delivery. If your designer gets paid in week 2 and the client doesn't pay until week 7, you're financing that work for five weeks. Profitability and cash flow are both important, but they're asking two different questions.</p>

## Why Agency Cash Timing Is Unpredictably Difficult

<p>Most agencies face four overlapping timing gaps that keep getting in the way:</p>

### The terms gap

<p>You land a $20,000 monthly retainer. Great! But when does the payment actually hit? If you bill in advance on the 1st, your cash arrives early. If you bill after the month closes, that retainer might not clear for another 30 days. A 50/30/20 milestone project structure similarly spreads out the cash differently than a single upfront deposit.</p>

### The late-payment gap

<p>A net-30 invoice <em>says</em> it's due 30 days after you send it. That doesn't mean your bank will actually see the money on day 30. I've seen clients who always pay around day 45. I've also seen "reliable" clients ghost us for 60+ days. Record both the due date <em>and</em> what you're expecting the client to actually do. If you've never collected from a prospect, don't assume they'll pay on 30-day terms in your forecast.</p>

### The pass-through gap

<p>This is a silent killer. A marketing agency might pay $18,000 to a media platform in week 2, and the client reimburses in week 5. The agency makes zero margin on that, sure, but that $18,000 is still gone from the bank for three weeks. You're treating it like a wash when it's actually working capital. Track the media spend and reimbursement as separate events. Or, if the contract allows, push for direct billing to the client.</p>

### The payroll gap

<p>Payroll, taxes, benefits, freelancer payments: these don't bend to client behavior. They're fixed dates. If you run payroll biweekly, you're liable every other week. A client payment that slips can cascade into a <a href="/blog/will-i-make-payroll" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">payroll shortfall</a> if you didn't account for the timing gap.</p>

<p><strong>Crucial:</strong> Don't count the same payment twice. An invoice in receivables and a matching bank deposit aren't two inflows. They're one. When the deposit clears, mark the forecasted receipt as settled.</p>

## How to Build a 13-Week Cash Flow Forecast for an Agency

### Step 1: Start with real, usable bank cash

<p>Check your accounts and only count what's actually available. Exclude client funds that are restricted, uncleared transfers, undrawn credit lines, and anything you're not legally able to move.</p>

### Step 2: Schedule receipts by *clearing* dates, not invoicing dates

<p>List retainer collections, project deposits, approved milestones, unpaid invoices, and reimbursements as separate items with client IDs.</p>

<p>Advance-billed? An invoice on November 1 might clear on November 3. Arrears-billed? That November retainer doesn't hit your bank until December. A $100,000 project billed 50/30/20? The deposit, approval milestone, and final payment all have different dates. Don't schedule them all on contract signing.</p>

<p>Review the last few payments from each of your material clients. If your net-30 clients consistently pay at day 45, use <em>that</em> in your baseline unless you have reason to believe they've changed behavior.</p>

### Step 3: Schedule payments on *actual debit dates*

<p>When is cash actually leaving the bank? When your payroll runs, when vendor invoices are paid, when ad platforms charge the account. Keep agency-funded ad spend separate from its reimbursement. If the client pays the platform directly? Neither transaction appears in your agency's cash forecast.</p>

### Step 4: Calculate every week's closing cash and identify the danger week

<p>Run the formula for each week and carry that balance forward. What's your minimum cash floor? Set it based on: one complete payroll cycle, essential operating payments, and some margin for uncertainty.</p>

<p>Cash headroom = Projected closing cash − Minimum cash floor</p>

<p>Find the <strong>lowest projected closing balance</strong> in your 13-week view. Compare it to the floor.</p>

### Step 5: Update from actuals every single week

<p>Each week, pull the bank statement, match cleared payments to invoices/expenses, and roll the forecast forward one week. Compare it to last week's forecast.</p>

<p><strong>Why?</strong> Because that variance tells you what assumptions were wrong. Was it a client who paid late? A vendor who changed payment dates? A milestone that got delayed? You're learning about your cash flow every time you update.</p>

## Three Agency Scenarios to Stress-Test

### Scenario 1: Your biggest client pays 21 days late

<p>Take one of your major expected receipts and push it three weeks later. Recalculate. What's your lowest balance now? Which payroll runs are now exposed?</p>

<p>If the model breaks, call that client's accounts payable team before you've delivered work for that month. Explore partial payments or revised billing cadence.</p>

### Scenario 2: A major retainer gets canceled

<p>If the client gives 30 days' notice, remove the revenue after the notice period from the forecast, not money you've already collected. Now ask: how many of your costs actually disappear? Freelancers can roll off quickly. Fixed payroll, software, rent: those stay. A lost $20,000 retainer doesn't magically save $20,000 in cash costs. Stress-test the whole thing.</p>

### Scenario 3: The agency fronts a $25,000 media campaign

<p>Client pays the platform directly? Great. But if the agency has to pay $25,000 in week 2 and gets reimbursed in week 6? That's four weeks of exposure. Test alternatives: advance client funding, split the supplier payment, or switch to direct billing.</p>

<p>For big decisions, run three scenarios: baseline, downside (clients pay late), and upside (clients pay early). Don't quietly add hypothetical new business to cover a known liquidity hole.</p>
