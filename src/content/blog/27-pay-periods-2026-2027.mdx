## Does 2026 Have 27 Pay Periods?

<p>Some do, some don't. A Friday biweekly schedule starting January 2, 2026 is the main one that can hit 27 in 2026.</p>

<p>Here's how it works:</p>

<p>If you pay on every other Friday, starting January 2, 2026, your normal schedule runs like this:</p>

<ul>
  <li>January 2, 2026</li>
  <li>January 16, 2026</li>
  <li>January 30, 2026</li>
  <li>And every 14 days after that</li>
  <li>December 4, 2026</li>
  <li>December 18, 2026</li>
  <li>January 1, 2027</li>
</ul>

<p>So the 27th scheduled payday lands on January 1, 2027.</p>

<p>But January 1 is New Year's Day, a <a href="https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/k8.htm" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">federal banking holiday</a>. If your payroll provider pays that run on the previous business day, you're hitting Thursday, December 31, 2026 instead.</p>

<p>That means December 2026 becomes a <strong>three-paycheck month</strong>:</p>

<p><strong>December 4 → December 18 → December 31</strong></p>

<p>And suddenly, 2026 has 27 actual pay dates.</p>

<p>This isn't just theory. The <a href="https://www.gsa.gov/buy-through-us/shared-services/hr-and-payroll-services/payroll-calendars/2026-payroll-calendar" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">U.S. General Services Administration's 2026 payroll calendar</a> lists December 4, December 18, and December 31 as official EFT pay dates.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://about.usps.com/postal-bulletin/2025/pb22692/html/info_002.htm" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">USPS 2026 pay schedule</a> also starts with January 2 and includes December 31.</p>

<p>But here's the catch: <strong>not every employer has 27 paydays in 2026.</strong> These are specific payroll systems, not a nationwide rule for everyone.</p>

### What about employees paid on Thursdays?

<p>January 1, 2026 fell on a Thursday, not a Friday.</p>

<p>If your payroll runs every other Thursday and your schedule includes January 1 and December 31, that year has 27 <em>scheduled</em> dates.</p>

<p>But if your January 1 payment got moved to December 31, 2025, that payment belongs to 2025.</p>

<p>So for your actual calendar, 2026 might only have 26 pay dates.</p>

<p><strong>The key is to count the dates you're actually paid, not just the recurring day of the week.</strong></p>

## Does 2027 Have 27 Pay Periods?

<p>It can. If your payroll uses a Friday schedule where January 1, 2027 is a nominal payday, 2027 could easily have 27 pay dates.</p>

<p>Here's why: January 1, 2027 is a Friday, and so is December 31, 2027.</p>

<p>A biweekly schedule starting on January 1 gives you 27 nominal pay dates in that calendar year.</p>

<p>Here's the math:</p>

<ul>
  <li>A normal year has 365 days</li>
  <li>26 biweekly periods = 364 days (26 × 14)</li>
  <li>That leaves 1 extra day</li>
</ul>

<p>So if your first payday is January 1, you've got room for a 27th on December 31.</p>

<p>But here's where it gets tricky: the New Year's Day banking holiday means that date might not actually be paid on January 1, 2027.</p>

<p>You have two options depending on your company's policy:</p>

<p><strong>Option 1: Pay on December 31, 2026 (early)</strong></p>

<p>The January 1 payroll runs on December 31 instead. The cash leaves the bank in 2026.</p>

<p>For this schedule: 2026 gets 27 pay dates, 2027 gets 26.</p>

<p><strong>Option 2: Pay on January 4, 2027 (later)</strong></p>

<p>The January 1 payment waits until Monday, January 4.</p>

<p>Same schedule now gives you: 2026 has 26 pay dates, 2027 has 27.</p>

<p><strong>Important:</strong> A holiday doesn't automatically mean you <em>can</em> or <em>should</em> pay early or late. Your employer's policy, state laws, and banking rules all apply.</p>

### Why you might see conflicting information

<p>You'll probably find articles saying "2026 has 27 pay periods" and others saying "2027 has 27 pay periods." Both could be true.</p>

<p>They're just describing the same Friday schedule from different angles:</p>

<ul>
  <li>One counts the nominal payday (January 1, 2027 belongs to 2027)</li>
  <li>Another counts the actual pay date (paid early on December 31, 2026)</li>
</ul>

<p>The question that actually matters:</p>

<p><strong>When will the January 1, 2027 payroll actually be paid?</strong></p>

<p>That's what determines which year's calendar includes it.</p>

<p>For employees, that's important: your extra paycheck isn't a bonus, it's pay that would normally have arrived in January.</p>

<p>For employers, it affects the cash you're asked to pay out of your bank that year, not necessarily your annual labor costs.</p>

## What Does a 27th Payday Mean for Your Take-Home Pay?

<p>A 27-payday year affects your pay schedule, deductions, and total compensation, but not all in the way you might expect.</p>

### If you're paid hourly

<p>Hourly employees get paid for the hours they worked during each pay period.</p>

<p>That 27th paycheck isn't a bonus. It's pay that would normally have arrived in January.</p>

<p>Overtime calculations, withholding, and other deductions still apply based on the actual hours and rules.</p>

### If you're on a salary

<p>Things get a bit more complicated.</p>

<p>Consider a $78,000 annual salary:</p>

<p><strong>Neither approach is universally required by law.</strong> Your employer could keep the established biweekly rate ($3,000/check), adjust under a valid contract amendment, or use some other compliant method.</p>

<p>It depends on your employment agreement, what state laws allow, and your company's payroll policy.</p>

<p>Before changing how salary is paid, employers need to check wage laws and exemption requirements. Just calculating the annualized rate doesn't tell you if the employee qualifies for salary-basis overtime exemption.</p>

### What about deductions?

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">If you receive an additional paycheck, per-paycheck deductions might change:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Health insurance premiums</li>
  <li>401(k) contributions</li>
  <li>Flexible spending accounts</li>
  <li>Other benefit elections</li>
  <li>Employer retirement contributions or matching</li>
</ul>

<p>Not all deductions have to be taken 27 times.</p>

<p><a href="https://usbenefits.microsoft.com/us/en/27-pay-periods-for-hourly-associates-in-2026.html" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Microsoft</a>, for instance, has a policy for eligible hourly associates where certain benefit deductions stay across 26 paychecks, while retirement contributions are handled differently.</p>

<p><strong>Check with your employer.</strong> Don't assume all deductions will stay the same just because you're getting an extra paycheck.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-401k-and-profit-sharing-plan-contribution-limits" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">Annual retirement contribution limits</a> are tied to the tax year, not your paycheck count: getting 27 payments doesn't automatically increase those limits.</p>

## How to Build a Payroll Cash Flow Forecast

<p>Payroll cash needs to be planned by actual payment date, not averaged into months.</p>

<p>A <a href="/blog/what-is-a-13-week-cash-flow-forecast" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">13-week cash flow forecast</a> is particularly helpful because it shows how payroll cash outflows stack up against customer receipts, vendor payments, loan draws, and other obligations.</p>

### Here's how to build it:

<p><strong>1. Start with your current cash position</strong></p>

<p>Pull the reconciled cash balance across all accounts that can be used for operations.</p>

<p>If some funds are restricted or pledged elsewhere, factor that in.</p>

<p><strong>2. Add actual payroll dates</strong></p>

<p>Import the dates from your payroll calendar, whether that means December 4, 18, and 31, or January 4 for 2027.</p>

<p>Record each run as a separate cash outflow, don't lump them into monthly totals.</p>

<p><strong>3. Include the full employer cash requirement</strong></p>

<p className="mb-2 font-medium text-foreground">Gross pay isn't all you're paying out. Add:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Payroll taxes (your share, plus any timing differences)</li>
  <li>Benefit payments (health insurance, etc.)</li>
  <li>Retirement contributions</li>
  <li>Payroll fees and admin costs</li>
  <li>Other employment-related cash outflows</li>
</ul>

<p>Note the exact date each one is due.</p>

<p><strong>4. Line it up against collections</strong></p>

<p>Build in expected customer payments. Remember, profitable businesses can still have cash shortages if customers pay after payroll is due.</p>

<p>A simple cash flow timeline might look like this:</p>

<p>Before the year wraps up, review these items with your payroll team:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Confirm your payroll frequency.</strong> Biweekly? Weekly? Semimonthly? Monthly?</li>
  <li><strong>Verify your anchor payday.</strong> What day does your 26th payment normally land on? Use actual company dates, not a generic national calendar.</li>
  <li><strong>Check January 1, 2027.</strong> Does that nominal payday move to December 31, 2026 or January 4, 2027 (or another date)?</li>

  <li><strong>Check salaried pay calculations.</strong> If a 27th payday changes how you calculate or distribute salary, make sure it follows employment agreements and wage laws.</li>
  <li><strong>Review benefit and retirement deductions.</strong> How will annual elections, per-paycheck premiums, and employer matches be handled in a 27-paycheck year?</li>
  <li><strong>Update your cash forecast.</strong> Add each payroll run as a discrete cash outflow. Include all employer-side costs and timing.</li>
  <li><strong>Tell your employees.</strong> If their payment dates are changing, communicate that in advance.</li>
</ol>

<p>The goal is to spot and handle any calendar issues before year-end processing.</p>
