Sounds impossible, right?

Both can be correct.

It's one of the more important distinctions in business finance. Having enough current assets to cover your short-term liabilities doesn't necessarily mean you'll actually have enough cash to pay your employees, suppliers, or lenders when their payments are due.

Net working capital is one of the fundamental metrics for understanding a company's short-term financial position. But here's the thing: calculating it is only the beginning. Understanding how that number translates into actual cash availability is what makes it useful for real financial decision-making.

In this guide, we'll cover how to calculate net working capital, what positive and negative working capital mean, how different business models affect working capital requirements, and how to use the metric alongside a <a href="/blog/what-is-a-13-week-cash-flow-forecast" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">13-week cash flow forecast</a>.

## What Is Net Working Capital (NWC)?

Net working capital (NWC) is simply the difference between a company's current assets and its current liabilities. It's what measures the short-term financial resources a business has relative to its short-term obligations.

Current assets generally include resources expected to be converted into cash, sold, or consumed within one year or the normal operating cycle, whichever is longer. Current liabilities represent obligations classified as payable within that period.

In plain English, net working capital helps answer a fundamental question:

**Does your business have enough short-term assets to cover its short-term financial obligations?**

A company with $150,000 in current assets and $100,000 in current liabilities has $50,000 in net working capital.

Now here's where it gets interesting: that does not mean the company has $50,000 available to spend.

Some of those assets might be outstanding customer invoices. Others might be inventory that hasn't been sold yet or expenses that have already been prepaid.

The distinction between owning an asset and having spendable cash becomes particularly important when managing payroll, supplier payments, debt repayments, and business growth.

## What Is the Net Working Capital Formula?

The standard net working capital formula is straightforward:

**Net Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities**

Both components are found on a company's balance sheet.

Current assets represent short-term economic resources owned or controlled by the business.

Cash is immediately available. Accounts receivable depend on customers paying. Inventory generally needs to be sold before it becomes cash. Prepaid expenses represent future economic benefits rather than spendable funds.

This is why two businesses with identical net working capital can have significantly different financial risk.

An upcoming payroll payment requires cash. Deferred revenue, on the other hand, generally requires the business to deliver a product or service rather than repay the entire amount in cash, although refunds and fulfillment costs may still create cash obligations.

Understanding the composition of your working capital is just as important as understanding its total.

## What Is a Good Net Working Capital Position?

Here's the truth: there is no universal amount of net working capital that every business should maintain.

A company generating $100,000 in annual revenue has very different working capital requirements from one generating $10 million. Business models, operating expenses, payment terms, seasonality, and access to financing all influence the appropriate level.

A current ratio between 1.5 and 2.0 is sometimes used as a general reference point, but don't treat it as a universal financial target.

A business with a current ratio of 2.5 could still experience cash shortages if its customers consistently pay late.

Meanwhile, a subscription business with a current ratio below 1.0 could maintain adequate liquidity because customers pay upfront and a large portion of its current liabilities consists of deferred revenue.

Rather than focusing exclusively on a benchmark, finance teams should evaluate:

- How quickly accounts receivable convert into cash.
- How much inventory is tied up in the operating cycle.
- When supplier payments, payroll, and taxes become due.
- How predictable incoming cash receipts are.
- Whether sufficient cash reserves exist to absorb unexpected expenses.
- Whether projected cash balances remain above the company's minimum operating cash threshold.

A healthy working capital position is one that supports the company's operating needs without unnecessarily tying up capital.

## Positive vs. Negative Net Working Capital

Net working capital can be positive, negative, or zero. Each result provides a different starting point for evaluating financial health.

### Positive net working capital

Positive net working capital occurs when current assets exceed current liabilities.

For example:

- Current assets: $150,000
- Current liabilities: $100,000
- Net working capital: $50,000

This can indicate that a business has sufficient short-term resources to cover its obligations.

However, don't assume that excessively high working capital is always desirable.

A business holding large amounts of unsold inventory or overdue accounts receivable may report substantial positive working capital while struggling to generate cash.

The objective is not necessarily to maximize net working capital. It is to maintain an efficient working capital position that supports operations and growth.

### Negative net working capital

Negative net working capital occurs when current liabilities exceed current assets.

For example:

- Current assets: $80,000
- Current liabilities: $110,000
- Net working capital: −$30,000

On paper, this may indicate that the business faces short-term liquidity pressure.

Potential causes include declining cash reserves, growing supplier balances, slow customer collections, upcoming debt repayments, or operating losses.

But here's where it gets nuanced: negative net working capital is not automatically a sign of financial distress.

### When negative working capital can be a good thing

Consider a subscription software company that collects annual payments upfront.

A customer purchases a $12,000 annual subscription.

The SaaS company receives $12,000 in cash immediately, but it recognizes the subscription revenue over the service period. Under a simplified monthly recognition model, after the first month it has recognized $1,000 in revenue, leaving $11,000 in deferred revenue.

## Net Working Capital vs. Operating Net Working Capital

You may have encountered different formulas for net working capital.

Some include cash and short-term debt. Others exclude them.

The difference generally comes down to whether you are measuring overall short-term financial position or the amount of capital tied up in business operations.

### Standard net working capital

**NWC = Current Assets − Current Liabilities**

Standard net working capital includes current financial assets and liabilities alongside operating assets and liabilities.

It is useful for evaluating a company's overall short-term financial position.

### Operating net working capital

**Operating NWC = Operating Current Assets − Operating Current Liabilities**

A common simplified calculation is:

**Operating NWC = Accounts Receivable + Inventory + Other Operating Current Assets − Accounts Payable − Accrued Operating Expenses − Other Operating Current Liabilities**

Operating net working capital typically excludes cash, non-operating short-term investments, and interest-bearing financing debt.

The treatment of certain items, such as taxes, leases, and other liabilities, can vary depending on the purpose of the analysis.

## How Does a Change in Net Working Capital Affect Cash Flow?

Changes in operating working capital help explain why a company's reported profit can differ from the cash generated by its operations.

The formula is:

**Change in Operating NWC = Ending Operating NWC − Beginning Operating NWC**

All else being equal:

- An increase in operating net working capital generally absorbs cash.
- A decrease in operating net working capital generally releases cash.

The corresponding working capital adjustment to operating cash flow has the opposite sign.

## How to Forecast Working Capital Over 13 Weeks

A 13-week cash flow forecast translates balance sheet positions and expected business activity into a weekly view of incoming and outgoing cash.

It helps finance teams understand whether receivables, inventory sales, and other cash sources will become available in time to meet upcoming obligations.

The process can be broken down into five steps.

### Step 1: Start with actual available cash

Use your current available bank balances rather than total current assets or accounting profit.

Exclude restricted cash that cannot be used for ordinary operating expenses.

### Step 2: Forecast accounts receivable collections

Review your open invoices and estimate when each customer will actually pay.

Contractual payment terms provide a starting point, but historical customer payment behavior often gives a more realistic collection forecast.

If a customer consistently pays invoices after 45 days, forecasting their receipt at 30 days may overstate your near-term cash position.

### Step 3: Schedule operating payments

Map accounts payable, payroll, rent, taxes, debt repayments, and other expenses to their expected payment weeks.

For inventory-based businesses, include supplier purchases, production costs, and replenishment requirements.

Avoid counting a prepaid expense as another cash outflow if the payment has already been made.

### Step 4: Calculate weekly closing cash

Use the following formula:

**Closing Cash = Opening Cash + Cash Inflows − Cash Outflows**

Each week's closing cash becomes the next week's opening cash.

### Step 5: Monitor your lowest projected cash balance

Instead of only reviewing ending cash, ask:

- What is the lowest projected cash balance over the next 13 weeks?
- Which week does it occur?
- Does it fall below our minimum operating cash threshold?
- Which customer payments or expenses are responsible?
- What happens if a major customer pays late?

## How Working Capital Differs Across Business Models

Different businesses convert their operating assets into cash at different speeds.

Understanding your operating model is important when interpreting net working capital.

### Working capital for SaaS companies

SaaS companies can benefit from collecting annual subscription payments upfront.

However, that upfront cash needs to support future service delivery. Rapid hiring, infrastructure expenses, and declining renewal rates can change the company's liquidity position even when subscription revenue appears strong.

### Working capital for agencies

Agencies often pay employees and contractors before collecting customer invoices.

An agency operating on Net 30 or Net 60 payment terms may effectively finance its clients' work for weeks.

This makes customer concentration, collection behavior, and payroll timing particularly important.

### Working capital for inventory-based businesses

E-commerce, retail, and manufacturing businesses often commit cash to inventory before generating revenue.

A profitable product can still create working capital pressure if inventory remains unsold for an extended period.

For these businesses, the cash conversion cycle is an important complementary metric.

## How to Improve Working Capital Without Sacrificing Growth

Improving working capital management is not simply about increasing current assets or reducing current liabilities.

It is about making cash available more efficiently while maintaining the company's ability to operate and grow.

### 1. Collect customer payments faster

Reducing the time customers take to pay can release cash tied up in accounts receivable.

Practical strategies include invoicing promptly, offering convenient payment methods, introducing milestone-based billing, collecting deposits, and following up before invoices become overdue.

### 2. Optimize supplier payment terms

Negotiating longer payment terms can help align supplier payments with customer collections.

For example, moving from Net 15 to Net 30 may provide additional operating flexibility.

However, extending agreed payment terms is different from paying suppliers late. Consistently missing contractual payment dates can damage supplier relationships and create additional costs.

### 3. Reduce unnecessary inventory

Businesses should review inventory turnover, slow-moving products, and purchasing commitments.

Reducing excess inventory can free up cash, provided it does not create stock shortages or disrupt customer demand.

### 4. Maintain an appropriate cash reserve

The objective is not to deploy every available dollar into growth.

Maintaining an <a href="/blog/business-cash-reserves" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">appropriate operating cash buffer</a> can help absorb late customer payments, unexpected expenses, and seasonal revenue fluctuations.

That reserve should reflect your actual financial obligations and forecast uncertainty rather than an arbitrary percentage of revenue.

### 5. Improve your cash conversion cycle

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) measures how long cash is tied up in the operating cycle.

**Cash Conversion Cycle = Days Inventory Outstanding + Days Sales Outstanding − Days Payable Outstanding**

Reducing the cycle can help businesses recover the cash committed to their operations more quickly.

## Spreadsheet vs. Automated Working Capital Monitoring

Many founders and finance teams calculate working capital using spreadsheets.

Spreadsheets are useful, flexible, and often the right starting point. They allow businesses to understand the underlying calculations and customize their financial models.

The challenge is maintaining those models as the financial position changes.

Every customer payment, new invoice, supplier bill, or unexpected expense can affect the cash forecast. When updates depend entirely on manual entry, the forecast can become outdated between reporting cycles.

## Final Thoughts: Working Capital Is a Starting Point, Not a Cash Forecast

Net working capital is a fundamental financial metric.

It helps founders and finance teams understand short-term financial position, evaluate operating efficiency, and identify capital tied up in business operations.

But it cannot tell you everything.

It cannot tell you whether your largest customer will pay next Tuesday. It cannot show you whether two payroll cycles will fall before your next major collection. And it cannot identify the lowest point your bank balance will reach over the next 13 weeks.

For that, you need a <a href="/blog/what-is-cash-flow-forecasting" className="font-medium text-primary underline-offset-4 hover:underline">forward-looking cash flow forecast</a>.

The most useful approach is to combine the balance sheet, working capital analysis, and weekly cash flow forecasting into a continuous financial planning process.

Your working capital tells you what you have. Your cash flow forecast tells you when you'll have it available as cash.
